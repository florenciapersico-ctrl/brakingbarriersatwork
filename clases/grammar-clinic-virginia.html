One · Warm-up

Six sentences. Don't fix them yet.

Read them once, out loud if you can. The only job right now is to notice where the sentence trips — not to repair it.

  1. I ask to my colleague.
  2. They who need going to use it should receive training.
  3. The name of the statuses are very friendly.
  4. In case it'll be necessary, we can change it.
  5. Would be okay taking the lead?
  6. What status represent Intuition's life cycle?

While you read

  • Which ones sound wrong immediately, and which ones almost pass?
  • Is there a word missing, or a word too many?
  • Is the trouble in a verb, a preposition, an article, the subject, or the word order of a question?

The ones that almost pass are the expensive ones. Those are the sentences that go out in an email and nobody corrects.

Two · Verb pattern

Ask doesn't take a preposition.

I ask to my colleague.

I ask my colleague.

I ask my colleague to check it.

The patterns

ask + person — I asked Sarah.

ask + person + to + verb — I asked Sarah to review the document.

ask + for + thing — I asked for more information.

ask + person + for + thing — I asked Sarah for more information.

Where the to actually belongs

This is the useful part. The to in this structure belongs to the second verb, never to the person:

I asked Virginia to explain the process.

Person: Virginia. Action: to explain. Once you hear it that way, ask to my colleague starts sounding like what it is — a to that lost its verb.

I asked to Sarah.

I asked to Sarah to send the file.

I asked Sarah.

I asked Sarah to send the file.

I asked Sarah for the file.

Fix these

  1. I asked to my manager about the deadline.
  2. We should ask to the client.
  3. She asked me helping her.
  4. I asked John a clarification.
Answers
  1. I asked my manager about the deadline.
  2. We should ask the client.
  3. She asked me to help her.
  4. I asked John for clarification.
Three · Articles and comparison

A workaround, not workaround.

like as workaround rather a proper process

more like a workaround than a proper process

It works as a workaround rather than as a proper process.

Three things went missing at once here, which is why the sentence feels harder to repair than it is. Take them one at a time.

One — workaround is countable

A singular countable noun almost never stands alone in English. It needs something in front of it.

It's workaround.

It's a workaround.

It's the workaround we discussed.

It's our workaround.

Two — rather than, complete

The structure is rather than + noun, verb or phrase. Not rather alone.

We use it as a temporary solution rather than a permanent process.

I'd call it a workaround rather than a solution.

Three — more like X than Y

This is the one I'd like you to steal. It's how you say something is not quite what it claims to be, without accusing anybody:

It's more like a workaround than a proper process.

At work that's a very useful sentence shape. You're describing your perception, not issuing a verdict — which makes it much easier for the other person to agree with you.

Same move, other contexts

This feels more like a temporary fix than a permanent solution.

I'd describe it as a workaround rather than an established process.

It's more like an internal guideline than an official policy.

Complete with a, an, the, more like… than or rather than

  1. It's ______ temporary solution.
  2. We should treat this as ______ exception ______ a general rule.
  3. It feels ______ workaround ______ actual solution.
  4. This isn't ______ process we normally follow.
Answers
  1. It's a temporary solution.
  2. … as an exception rather than a general rule.
  3. It feels more like a workaround than an actual solution.
  4. This isn't the process we normally follow.
Four · Future

Going to never travels without be.

Two of your sentences broke in the same place, which is how I know this one isn't a slip — it's a habit.

they who need going to use it

who don't gonna use it

The full structure

subject + be + going to + base verb

I am going to use it. · She is going to use it. · They are going to use it.

In the negative

They aren't going to use it.

They don't going to use it.

They don't gonna use it.

The be is what carries the negative. Don't has nothing to do here, because going isn't the main verb — use is.

Gonna is pronunciation, not grammar

You'll hear it constantly: I'm gonna call him. They're gonna change it. That's just how going to sounds in fast informal speech. Notice what survives even there:

They're gonna use it.

They don't gonna use it.

The be never disappears. And in anything you write at work, use the full form: They are going to use it.

Those who, not they who

They who exists in English roughly the way aquellos que exists in Spanish — technically readable, but nobody at your office says it.

Those who are going to use it should receive training.

People who are going to use it should receive training.

And these two don't mean the same thing

Employees who need to use the system… — using it is a requirement.

Employees who are going to use the system… — they intend to, or they're expected to. In your original sentence you had both structures fighting for the same slot. Pick the meaning first, then the structure follows.

Rewrite

  1. People who going to participate should register.
  2. Those who don't going to attend should let us know.
  3. Employees who need going to use the platform need access.
  4. She's going use the new system tomorrow.
Answers
  1. People who are going to participate should register.
  2. Those who aren't going to attend should let us know.
  3. Employees who need to use the platform need access.
  4. She's going to use the new system tomorrow.
Five · Precaution

In case doesn't take will.

in case it'll be necessary

in case it's necessary

in case it becomes necessary

The future is already in the sentence — it lives in the main clause. The in case part stays in the present, even though it clearly refers to something that hasn't happened yet.

I'll save a copy in case we need it later.

I'll prepare another version in case we'll need it.

Keep the document in case it'll be necessary.

I'll prepare another version in case we need it.

Keep the document in case it's necessary.

In case and if are not interchangeable

This is worth more to you than the will rule, honestly, because getting it wrong changes what you're saying.

If — condition

I'll call the technician if the system stops working.

I call only when the problem actually happens.

In case — precaution

I'll save the technician's number in case the system stops working.

I'm preparing now, because it might happen later.

If or in case?

  1. Take your laptop ______ we need to work after the meeting.
  2. ______ we need more time, we'll extend the deadline.
  3. Save a backup ______ something goes wrong.
  4. ______ the client changes the requirements, we'll adapt the process.
Answers
  1. in case — you take it as a precaution.
  2. If — the extension depends on the condition.
  3. in case — precaution again.
  4. If — we adapt only when it happens.
Six · Determiners

Every counts one at a time.

use it every status

What the correction is depends on what you meant, and both are useful to have:

use it for every status

use it at every stage

After every, one thing

every status · every stage · every employee · every meeting

Never every statuses, never every employees. Every looks at the group and points at the members one by one.

every status

Each individual status.

We need a description for every status.

all statuses

The group as a whole.

We reviewed all the statuses.

Choose

  1. Every employee / employees needs access.
  2. We need to review every stages / stage.
  3. All status / statuses should be documented.
  4. Each users / user receives an email.
Answers
  1. Every employee
  2. every stage
  3. all statuses
  4. each user
Seven · Agreement

The verb agrees with the subject,
not with the closest noun.

the name of the statuses are very friendly and common

The name of the statuses is very friendly and common.

The names of the statuses are very friendly and common.

Both are correct English. Which one you want depends on whether you're talking about one name or several — so the first question isn't grammatical, it's factual.

Find the real subject

In The name of the statuses is clear, the subject is the name. Of the statuses is extra information about which name; it has no say in the verb.

The list of problems is long.

The purpose of these statuses is clear.

The problems on the list are serious.

The statuses are easy to understand.

Why it happens

The longer the phrase between subject and verb, the harder your ear works against you:

The description of all the different stages of the process is confusing.

Your ear hears stages and process right before the verb. The subject is description, four words back. When the sentence gets long, cover the middle with your finger and read only the two ends.

Underline the real subject, then choose is or are

  1. The purpose of these meetings ______ clear.
  2. The names of the stages ______ confusing.
  3. The list of possible statuses ______ available online.
  4. The descriptions of the process ______ useful.
Answers
  1. The purpose … is
  2. The names … are
  3. The list … is
  4. The descriptions … are
Eight · Third person

The -s that disappears.

what status represent Intuition's life cycle

Which status represents this stage of Intuition's life cycle?

Why represents? Because status is it. In the present simple, everything is bare except that one form:

I represent · you represent · we represent · they represent

but: it represents · the status represents

The system shows the current stage.

The process includes four steps.

The document explains the workflow.

This one you know perfectly well. It goes missing under pressure, not out of ignorance — which is exactly why drilling it is worth the five minutes.

Choose

  1. The status represent / represents a completed task.
  2. These statuses represents / represent different stages.
  3. The system include / includes four categories.
  4. Our guidelines explains / explain the process.
Answers
  1. represents — one status.
  2. represent — plural subject.
  3. includes — the system, singular.
  4. explain — guidelines, plural.
Nine · Word order

Questions have their own word order.

This is the section that will change the most in how you sound, because questions are what you ask all day and the mistake is audible immediately.

A direct question inverts

when status is normally expected to be used?

When is the status normally expected to be used?

question word + be + subject

When is the status used? · Why is this option necessary? · Where are these files stored?

An embedded question doesn't

The moment the question sits inside another sentence, it goes back to normal order.

Can you explain when the status is normally expected to be used?

Can you explain when is the status normally expected to be used?

Direct

When is the status used?

Embedded

Do you know when the status is used?

Worth knowing: the embedded version is also the polite one. Could you tell me when the document is updated? lands softer than the direct question, which is often what you want with a client.

When the question word is the subject

Here's the distinction hiding behind your sentence about the statuses.

Which status represents…?

Which status represents the final stage?

No does. Which status is the subject — it's the thing doing the representing. You're trying to identify the status.

What does the status represent?

What does this status represent?

Here the subject is this status, so you need does + base verb — and the -s moves onto does. You already know the status; you want its meaning.

What does this status represents?

One -s per question. Does already took it.

Question clinic

  1. When the meeting is going to start?
  2. What this status means?
  3. Which option represent the final stage?
  4. Do you know when is the deadline?
  5. Why this process is necessary?
Answers
  1. When is the meeting going to start?
  2. What does this status mean?
  3. Which option represents the final stage?
  4. Do you know when the deadline is? — embedded, no inversion.
  5. Why is this process necessary?
Ten · Polite questions

Would needs someone to be okay.

Would be okay taking the lead…?

English almost never lets a verb sit without a subject. Spanish does — ¿Estaría bien…? is a complete question. English needs to say who or what would be okay. You have two options, and they don't ask the same thing.

Would it be okay to + verb?

Would it be okay to take the lead?

Is this action acceptable? You're asking about the situation, not about the person.

Would you be okay with + -ing?

Would you be okay with taking the lead?

Are you comfortable doing it? Now you're asking the person directly.

Would it be okay to change the deadline?

Would it be okay to mention this during the meeting?

Would you be okay with presenting this section?

Would you be okay with moving the meeting?

In a meeting, the first one is safer when you don't want to put someone on the spot. The second one is the right choice when the answer genuinely depends on how they feel about it.

Complete

  1. Would ______ be okay to postpone the meeting?
  2. Would you be okay ______ presenting first?
  3. Would it be okay ______ contact the client?
  4. Would she be okay ______ taking responsibility for this task?
Answers
  1. it
  2. with
  3. to
  4. with

Twelve · A distinction that matters

Wrong, or just not what we'd say?

Not everything that sounds unusual is a grammar error, and I don't want you treating the two the same way. Some of the expressions in your material — take the ownership, descriptive by itself, proficient way, organize all — are perfectly well-built sentences. They're just not the words a proficient speaker would reach for there.

It's descriptive by itself.

The structure is fine: subject + be + adjective + prepositional phrase. Nothing is broken. The improvement is a vocabulary one — the label is self-explanatory is what someone would actually say. That's a different kind of note from this:

The labels is clear.

That one is broken.

Why I'm separating them

Grammar errors you fix with a rule, and they stay fixed. Naturalness you fix by collecting — hearing an expression, noting it, using it. Different problem, different work, different timeline. If you treat every awkward phrase as a mistake, you'll end up second-guessing sentences that were fine, and that hesitation costs you more in a meeting than the phrasing did.

Sixteen · Production

Explain the workflow.

Your company has introduced a new system with different statuses representing the stages of a project's life cycle. Explain it to a new colleague, in writing, in about 150–200 words.

Include at least

  • one ask + person + to + verb
  • one be going to + verb, and one in the negative
  • one sentence with in case
  • one every + singular noun
  • one long subject with of + plural noun
  • one Which…? question and one What does…? question
  • one When is…? question
  • one polite question with Would…?

We use several statuses to represent the different stages of the project's life cycle. The name of each status is designed to make its purpose easy to understand. People who are going to use the system regularly should become familiar with every status…

That's the opening. Keep going from there — and bring it to the next session even if you're not happy with it.

Seventeen · Self-correction

Before you hit send.

Twelve seconds, on anything that matters. Not on every message — you'd stop writing altogether.

Verb patterns

  • ask + person, with no to in front of them?
  • After need, did I use to + verb?
  • Is there an am / is / are in front of every going to?

Future

  • No will after in case?
  • Did I mean a precaution (in case) or a condition (if)?

Nouns

  • Does that singular countable noun have a / an / the / every / my / this?
  • Is the noun after every singular?

Agreement

  • What's the real subject — not the noun closest to the verb?
  • Singular or plural? Does the verb match it?
  • Third person singular: is the -s there?

Questions

  • Direct question — did I invert?
  • Embedded question — did I not invert?
  • Is the question word itself the subject? Then no do / does.

Would

  • Is there a subject after would?
  • Would it be okay to + verb, or would you be okay with + -ing?

Nineteen · Homework

From correction to automatic.

Write eight to ten sentences about a real process at your job — one you'd have to explain to somebody new. Not exercises: an explanation you could actually send.

Use all of these

  • ask someone to…
  • somebody is going to… and somebody isn't going to…
  • in case…
  • every + singular noun
  • the + singular noun + of + plural noun + singular verb
  • Which…? · What does…? · Would it be okay to…?

Then close this document and go back over what you wrote, marking the subject and the main verb of every sentence. That second pass is the actual homework — the writing is just what gives you something to look at.

The goal isn't I know the correction.

Anyone can learn a correction. What we're building is the half-second earlier: recognising the pattern while the sentence is still forming, before it comes out of your mouth.

That takes repetition on your own material, which is why the homework is about your job and not about a textbook. Bring it however it turns out. Half-finished and honest is more useful to me than polished and safe.
