# Advanced Work Presentations: Executives, Data, Virtual

Evidence labels used throughout:
- **[EXPERT]** = practitioner/expert opinion (Duarte, Knaflic, Winston, Abrahams, Minto, consultants). Most of this topic is expert opinion, not experimental evidence.
- **[RESEARCH]** = academic/peer-reviewed or research-based argument.
- **[VENDOR/BLOG]** = commercial or secondary source; treat with caution.
- **[DRAFTED]** = example English phrases written for this audience (Spanish-speaking professionals presenting in English), derived from the cited advice but not quoted from a source.

## 1. Presenting to executives: answer first, the executive summary slide, interruptions, time cuts, decision asks, appendix

### Takeaway
The best-sourced guidance here is Nancy Duarte's (HBR, October 2012; republished on Duarte.com). Plan as if your 30-minute slot has been cut to 5 minutes. Open with findings, recommendations and the call to action. Tell the room how you will split the time between summary and discussion. Keep about 10% of the content as summary slides and move everything else to an appendix you pull up when someone asks. Expect interruptions. All of this is expert opinion, not experimental evidence.

### Cited Findings
- [EXPERT] Duarte frames presenting to senior executives as "respecting their time and being prepared." Her five steps are: summarize up front, set expectations, create summary slides, give them what they asked for, and rehearse. — [Duarte blog, "How to effectively present to senior executives"](https://www.duarte.com/blog/how-to-effectively-present-to-senior-executives/)
- [EXPERT] The 5-minute test: "pretend your whole slot got cut to 5 minutes." Open with high-level findings, conclusions, recommendations and a call to action. Supporting data and peripheral material come after. — [Duarte blog](https://www.duarte.com/blog/how-to-effectively-present-to-senior-executives/)
- [EXPERT] Expect interruptions. Senior executives are impatient: "They'll just interrupt you before you finish your shtick." — [Duarte blog](https://www.duarte.com/blog/how-to-effectively-present-to-senior-executives/)
- [EXPERT] Set expectations at the start. Say you will spend the first few minutes on the summary and the rest on discussion. Executives are more likely to let you finish if they know their questions will get time. — [Duarte blog](https://www.duarte.com/blog/how-to-effectively-present-to-senior-executives/)
- [EXPERT] The appendix "10% rule": "If your appendix is 50 slides, create 5 summary slides." Let the group steer the discussion and pull up the relevant appendix slides when they want depth. — [Duarte blog](https://www.duarte.com/blog/how-to-effectively-present-to-senior-executives/)
- [EXPERT] Answer the request first. If you were invited for a specific issue (her example is a flooded plant), address it directly and quickly before anything else. — [Duarte blog](https://www.duarte.com/blog/how-to-effectively-present-to-senior-executives/)
- [EXPERT] Rehearse with a colleague who has gotten ideas adopted at the executive level. Ask whether the message comes through quickly, whether the summary slides can be skimmed, and whether anything expected is missing. — [Duarte blog](https://www.duarte.com/blog/how-to-effectively-present-to-senior-executives/)
- [EXPERT] An HBR Guides chapter, "Present Clearly and Concisely to Senior Executives," also says that presentations building to a big reveal do not work for executives, who want the bottom line immediately and often interrupt. (Seen through a search snippet only; the full text is paywalled.) — [O'Reilly / HBR Guide excerpt](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/harvard-business-review/9781633697829/11150/OEBPS/xhtml/12-chap3.xhtml)

### Inferences
- The "skip to the end" problem goes away if the end comes first. When a 30-minute slot shrinks to 5, the presenter delivers the summary slides and nothing else. Duarte's 5-minute test is effectively rehearsal for that scenario.
- A decision-oriented ask should go on the first or summary slide, not the last one. This follows from Duarte's "call to action up front."
- [DRAFTED] Ready-to-use phrases:
  - Answer first: "The bottom line is: we recommend X, which will deliver Y by Z. I need your approval on two things today."
  - Setting expectations: "I'll take five minutes to walk you through the summary, and then I'd like to spend the rest of our time on your questions and the decision."
  - Time cut: "I know we're short on time, so let me go straight to the recommendation and the decision we need from you." / "If you remember one thing, it's this: ..."
  - Being told to skip to the end: "Absolutely. The answer is X. The key reason is Y. Happy to go into the detail if useful."
  - Interruption with a deep-dive question: "Great question. I have the detail in the appendix; let me pull it up." / "Let me answer that briefly now and come back to it in the discussion."
  - Not knowing the answer: "I don't have that number with me. I'll confirm it and send it to you by end of day tomorrow."
  - Decision ask: "What I need from you today is a decision on A or B. We recommend A."
  - Bringing the discussion back: "To bring us back to the decision: are we aligned on moving forward with X?"

### Gaps
- No empirical study was found showing that answer-first outperforms build-up for executive audiences. The support is practitioner consensus (Duarte, HBR Guides, consulting practice).
- The "Create summary slides" section of the original 2012 HBR article could not be read directly because of the paywall. The details above come from Duarte's own blog version.
- No reliable source was found for a standard executive-summary slide template. A common practitioner pattern is SCQA on one slide (see section 4).

## 2. Pre-wiring: socializing ideas with stakeholders and sending pre-reads

### Takeaway
"Pre-wiring" means meeting key decision-makers one-on-one before the group meeting so the room holds no surprises. It is usually attributed to McKinsey through Ethan Rasiel's *The McKinsey Way*, and it parallels the Japanese practice of *nemawashi*. Sources describe both benefits (buy-in, a chance to fold in objections) and costs (time, ideas getting diluted). All sources found are secondary or practitioner blogs.

### Cited Findings
- [EXPERT, secondary] Summarizing *The McKinsey Way*: pre-wiring means discussing your findings and recommendations with key decision-makers individually, ahead of a group presentation, so you can secure their support and avoid surprise reactions. — [RightAttitudes, "Pre-wiring presentations for buy-in" (2008)](https://www.rightattitudes.com/2008/05/27/pre-wiring-presentations-for-buy-in)
- [EXPERT] Negotiation, compromise and new facts get built into the proposal before the presentation. — [RightAttitudes](https://www.rightattitudes.com/2008/05/27/pre-wiring-presentations-for-buy-in)
- [EXPERT] A practitioner argues that the goal is for the idea to survive after the meeting, which needs every other head nodding, not just the CEO's. — ["Pre-Wiring: The Art of Getting Stakeholder Buy-In", Unwritten Business Guide](https://unwrittenbusinessguide.substack.com/p/pre-wiring-the-art-of-getting-stakeholder)
- [EXPERT] Downsides: heavy time cost (one consultant spent about a month sharing findings), the risk that pre-wiring dilutes or kills the idea, and exposure to deliberate sabotage. — ["The downsides of pre-wiring", Unwritten Business Guide](https://unwrittenbusinessguide.substack.com/p/the-downsides-of-pre-wiring)
- [EXPERT] Simply meeting attendees beforehand can make them sympathetic to your position. A lighter version is a quick message to your manager asking for their opinion. — [The Effective Statistician, "How to prewire a meeting"](https://theeffectivestatistician.com/how-to-prewire-a-meeting/)
- [EXPERT] A commenter on Manager Tools argues that sending a document is not the same as a pre-wire, because much of the value is relationship building. — [Manager Tools comment](https://manager-tools.com/comment/292422)
- [EXPERT] The *nemawashi* parallel: meet each stakeholder one-on-one, share the proposal, and hear their concerns and suggested changes before the group meeting. — [Nemawashi seed description](https://git.medlab.host/ntnsndr/protocol-droid/raw/branch/main/seeds/nemawashi-informal-alignment.yaml) (low-authority source)

### Inferences
- A pre-read helps but does not replace a conversation. The comment above suggests pairing the two: send the pre-read, then have a 10–15 minute call with the most influential or most skeptical stakeholders.
- For non-native speakers, pre-wiring has an extra benefit: the hardest questions get asked in a low-stakes one-on-one, so the public meeting carries less linguistic and emotional load. (This is my inference; no source addresses it.)
- [DRAFTED] Ready-to-use phrases:
  - Asking for a pre-wire: "Before Thursday's meeting, could I get 15 minutes of your time to walk you through our recommendation and hear your concerns?"
  - Inviting pushback: "What would make this hard to support?" / "Is there anything here that would surprise your team?"
  - Pre-read email: "Attached is a two-page pre-read. The key decision we'll ask for is X. Please take a look at page 1 before the meeting; the rest is backup."
  - In the meeting: "As I discussed with some of you beforehand, ..." / "Thanks to input from [name], we've adjusted the timeline."

### Gaps
- I could not verify the McKinsey attribution against the primary book (*The McKinsey Way*, Rasiel, 1999) or a McKinsey source.
- No reliable evidence was found on how long a pre-read should be, or on the Amazon-style "six-pager plus silent reading" practice. Neither was researched here.

## 3. Data storytelling: Knaflic, Duarte, action titles, one number, walking through a chart

### Takeaway
Knaflic's *Storytelling with Data* gives six lessons: context, choose an appropriate display, eliminate clutter, focus attention, think like a designer, tell a story. Before building any charts, she has you reduce the message to a "3-minute story" and a one-sentence "Big Idea." Her team gives a four-step method for explaining a chart out loud (Alex Velez): skeleton, then metadata, then what to see, then the takeaway. Duarte's "glance test" holds that each slide should be understood in about 3 seconds, like a billboard. All of this is expert opinion.

### Cited Findings
- [EXPERT] The six lessons of *Storytelling with Data*: understand the context, choose an appropriate display, eliminate clutter, draw attention where you want the audience to focus, think like a designer, tell a story. — [Association Analytics summary](https://associationanalytics.com/blog/storytelling-with-data/); [O'Reilly book listing](https://oreilly.com/library/view/storytelling-with-data/9781119002253)
- [EXPERT] The 3-minute story asks: "if you had only three minutes to tell your audience what they need to know, what would you say?" The Big Idea is a single sentence that captures the core message. — [Association Analytics](https://associationanalytics.com/blog/storytelling-with-data/); [Blinkist summary](https://www.blinkist.com/en/books/storytelling-with-data-en)
- [EXPERT] Knaflic encourages storyboarding the message (beginning, middle, end) before creating charts. One reviewer's practice is to cut the story to 3 minutes, then 60 seconds, then a single sentence. — [SEP book review](https://sep.com/blog/book-review-storytelling-with-data/)
- [EXPERT] Focus attention with preattentive attributes (size, color, position), used sparingly and on purpose. To test a chart, ask a colleague where their eyes go first. — [Association Analytics](https://associationanalytics.com/blog/storytelling-with-data/)
- [EXPERT, secondary] Decluttering steps: remove chart borders, remove or lighten gridlines, remove data markers, clean up axis labels, label data directly instead of using legends. — [Skill summary of SWD approach](https://skillselion.com/skills/sammcj/agentic-coding/storytelling-with-data) (low-authority secondary)
- [EXPERT] Four steps for verbalizing a chart (Alex Velez, storytellingwithdata.com):
  1. Start with the skeleton. Say what the chart shows and read the title and axes aloud. "Resist the urge to talk about the shape of the data immediately."
  2. Cover the critical metadata, such as definitions, date ranges and methodology. "I like to think of this as verbalizing the footnotes in your design."
  3. Highlight what the audience should see: "Describe the shape of your data and any key insights."
  4. Conclude with the main takeaway: "What should your audience's final thought be about your graph?"
  — [SWD blog, "Verbalizing and explaining charts"](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/explaining-charts)
- [EXPERT] In live settings, the SWD team builds a graph one component at a time with animation, which static formats cannot do. — [SWD blog, "Animating data" (2018)](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/2018/9/12/animating-data)
- [EXPERT] The SWD podcast episode "Say it out loud" recommends practicing chart explanations aloud, which also helps you plan transitions. — [SWD podcast ep. 6](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/podcast/say-it-out-loud)
- [EXPERT] Duarte's glance test: "People should be able to comprehend each one in about three seconds." "Think of your slides as billboards." "An audience can't listen to your presentation and read detailed, text-heavy slides at the same time." (HBR, October 2012.) — [Small Wars Journal repost of Duarte's HBR piece](https://smallwarsjournal.com/blog/do-your-slides-pass-the-glance-test); [Duarte Glance Test tool](https://www.duarte.com/resources/guides-tools/the-glance-test/)
- [EXPERT, practitioner] Action titles state the slide's takeaway as a complete sentence (e.g., not "Market Analysis"). Consultants are trained so that someone can follow a deck by reading only the titles ("horizontal flow"). An action title containing "and" is a warning sign of more than one message. — [Deckary, consulting slides guide](https://deckary.com/blog/consulting-quality-slides); [StrategyU, consulting presentations](https://strategyu.co/consulting-presentations/) (commercial training sources)
- [EXPERT, practitioner] "Ghost deck" practice: draft the storyline as title-only placeholder slides before doing the analysis or design. — [Deckary](https://deckary.com/blog/pillar-consulting-presentations-guide)

### Inferences
- An executive data slide can combine three ideas: an action title (the Big Idea as a sentence), one highlighted data point in color with everything else in grey (focus attention), and enough simplicity to pass the 3-second glance test.
- The "one number that matters" idea fits Knaflic's guidance to focus attention and Duarte's billboard rule, but I found no primary source that coins the phrase. Treat it as a coaching heuristic.
- [DRAFTED] Phrases for walking through a chart, following Velez's four steps:
  1. Skeleton: "This chart shows monthly churn for our top 20 accounts. Time is on the horizontal axis, from January to September, and churn rate is on the vertical axis."
  2. Metadata: "Quick note on the definition: churn here means accounts that didn't renew within 30 days."
  3. What to see: "Notice the grey lines are flat, but look at the orange line: churn in the LATAM region doubled after the price change in May."
  4. Takeaway: "So the key takeaway is: the price change is driving churn in LATAM, and we recommend rolling it back there by Q1."
- [DRAFTED] Example action titles:
  - Topic title (weak): "Q3 Sales." Action title (strong): "Q3 sales grew 12%, driven entirely by Brazil."
  - "Customer support costs can drop 20% if we automate tier-1 tickets."
- [DRAFTED] Useful data-language chunks: "rose / fell / plateaued / spiked / bottomed out", "year over year", "compared to the same period last year", "this is a leading indicator of...", "the outlier here is...", "correlation, not necessarily causation".

### Gaps
- Nancy Duarte's *DataStory* (2019) and *Resonate* (the "what is / what could be" contour) were not researched here, so no cited findings are available. Fetch duarte.com/datastory if needed.
- The exact definition of Knaflic's Big Idea (that it should state a point of view, convey what's at stake, and be a complete sentence) comes from my background knowledge of the book and was not verified in this session.
- The primary storytellingwithdata.com page on decluttering was not retrieved. The declutter steps above come from secondary summaries.

## 4. Structure frameworks: Minto Pyramid / SCQA, What–So what–Now what, Problem–Solution–Benefit, signposting

### Takeaway
Minto's Pyramid Principle (developed at McKinsey) says to lead with the answer and then group the supporting arguments beneath it. SCQA (Situation–Complication–Question–Answer) frames the opening. Matt Abrahams (Stanford GSB) teaches What? So what? Now what? as a portable structure for speaking on your feet. Patrick Winston (MIT) recommends signposting ("verbal punctuation") and cycling (brief, then detailed, then summary). These are expert frameworks; none is empirically validated against the others.

### Cited Findings
- [EXPERT, secondary] Barbara Minto developed the Pyramid Principle at McKinsey in the 1960s. It leads with the answer and then supports it. SCQA usually frames the opening (slides 1–3) and the pyramid structures the supporting analysis. Some consultants use the simpler SCR (Situation–Complication–Resolution). — [Deckary, "The Pyramid Principle"](https://deckary.com/blog/pyramid-principle-consulting); [ModelThinkers, Pyramid Principle](https://modelthinkers.com/mental-model/225)
- [EXPERT] Abrahams' What / So what / Now what: what = the topic (process, product, point of view); so what = why it matters to listeners or the company; now what = the next step (action items, follow-up meeting, demo). Structure "gives us a place to start, it gives us a place to end." — [Stanford GSB Insights, "Quick Thinks: Tips for Speaking in Virtual Meetings" (April 2020)](https://gsb.stanford.edu/insights/quick-thinks-tips-speaking-virtual-meetings)
- [EXPERT] Abrahams calls the structure "a recipe," not a script. He describes a marketing manager who used it to fill in for a speaker with minutes of notice. — [Upworthy on Abrahams](https://www.upworthy.com/6-word-framework-stanford-professor-matt-abrahams/); [Entrepreneur article by Abrahams](https://entm.ag/SwHbek)
- [EXPERT] Abrahams' three ways to get the floor in a meeting: paraphrase what was said; ask a question ("I have a question about that point"); state an emotion ("That concerns me"). — [Stanford GSB Insights](https://gsb.stanford.edu/insights/quick-thinks-tips-speaking-virtual-meetings)
- [EXPERT] Winston's "cycling": "Deliver ideas first in brief, then in detail, then in summary." His "verbal punctuation" gives listeners who drifted off a way back in, e.g., "We have just finished talking about the first heuristic..." — [Cal Newport summary of Winston's "How to Speak"](https://calnewport.com/the-art-of-speaking/); [MIT OCW RES.TLL-005 How to Speak (2018)](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-tll-005-how-to-speak-january-iap-2018/pages/how-to-speak/)

### Inferences
- These frameworks fit together. SCQA or What/So what/Now what works for the opening or a 60-second answer, the Pyramid for the body and slide titles, and Problem–Solution–Benefit for a persuasive pitch. (I found no authoritative source for Problem–Solution–Benefit; it is a widely used sales and pitch pattern. See Gaps.)
- [DRAFTED] SCQA template for an executive opening:
  - S: "Over the last two years, our LATAM e-commerce revenue has grown 30% annually."
  - C: "But since March, delivery delays have doubled and NPS has dropped 15 points."
  - Q: "So the question is: how do we fix delivery before peak season?"
  - A: "We recommend switching to a regional carrier in Brazil and Mexico, at a cost of $400K, which pays back in six months."
- [DRAFTED] What / So what / Now what for an update: "What: the pilot finished last week with 85% adoption. So what: that's twice our target, which means we can roll it out without extra training budget. Now what: I'd like approval to launch in all five countries in November."
- [DRAFTED] Signposting phrases:
  - Roadmap: "I'll cover three things: where we are, what's changed, and what we need from you."
  - Transitions: "That covers the problem. Now let's look at the options." / "Moving on to the second point..."
  - Emphasis: "This is the critical point." / "If you remember one thing, ..."
  - Recap (verbal punctuation): "So far we've seen that X. Now, Y."
  - Return to structure: "Coming back to the decision we need..."

### Gaps
- Barbara Minto's primary text (*The Pyramid Principle*) was not consulted. Pyramid and SCQA details come from consulting-training blogs.
- No authoritative origin was found for Problem–Solution–Benefit.

## 5. Virtual presentations on Teams/Zoom: engagement, camera, screen-sharing hygiene, shorter segments, chat/Q&A

### Takeaway
The research-based foundation is Bailenson's (Stanford, 2021) theoretical argument for four causes of "Zoom fatigue": close-up eye gaze, cognitive load from nonverbal cues, self-view, and reduced mobility. Hiding self-view is one concrete fix. The paper says these are arguments that have not yet been tested. Specific engagement cadences (an interaction every 10–12 minutes, or every 90 seconds) come from vendor and practitioner sources and are not validated. Microsoft documents the presenter modes and the Focus mode in Teams.

### Cited Findings
- [RESEARCH, theoretical] Bailenson's "Nonverbal Overload: A Theoretical Argument for the Causes of Zoom Fatigue" (Technology, Mind, and Behavior, APA, 2021) proposes four causes: excessive close-up eye contact, cognitive load, constant self-view (seeing your own reflection increases self-criticism), and reduced mobility. The fixes include hiding self-view, using an external camera farther away so you can move, and periodic audio-only breaks. The paper states its arguments "require future experimentation to confirm." — [Bailenson, APA TMB (2021)](https://tmb.apaopen.org/pub/nonverbal-overload/release/1); [Stanford News, Feb 2021](https://news.stanford.edu/2021/02/23/four-causes-zoom-fatigue-solutions/)
- [VENDOR/BLOG] Keep content segments under 10 minutes and add an interactive element every 10–12 minutes. — [AhaSlides blog](https://ahaslides.com/blog/7-zoom-presentation-tips) (vendor of interaction tools; unverified)
- [EXPERT/opinion] A training-industry article says presenters should "interact with your audience in a different way every 90 seconds." — [IAPD, "Training in a virtual world: overcoming Zoom fatigue" (2021)](https://digital.iapd.org/issue/august-september-2021/training-in-a-virtual-world-overcoming-zoom-fatigue/)
- [VENDOR/BLOG; unverified] A claimed average attention window of 47 seconds, and a survey saying 46% pay less attention in video meetings. Neither source could be traced to the original study. Do not cite these as fact. — [AhaSlides](https://ahaslides.com/blog/7-zoom-presentation-tips); [Mr Porter](https://www.mrporter.com/journal/lifestyle/zoom-fatigue-video-conferencing-mental-health-work-from-home-1746548)
- [PLATFORM DOCS] Teams presenter modes (Standout, Side-by-side, Reporter) put your video over the shared content. They are available only on desktop; web and mobile attendees see the content and the video separately. You can pin the presenter toolbar. — [Lambton College Teams guide](https://lambtoncollege.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/ICD/pages/91651988/Microsoft+Teams+-+Presenter+Modes)
- [PLATFORM DOCS] The Teams presenter window (rolled out in 2024) shows up to four participants' video, raised hands, reactions and a content preview while you share your screen. — [Microsoft 365 Insider blog](https://insider.microsoft365.com/en-us/blog/teams-enhancements-to-the-presenter-window-while-screensharing)
- [BLOG] Use Windows Focus Assist or Do Not Disturb (or the macOS equivalent) to silence notifications while presenting. Teams' own Focus mode hides in-meeting distractions. — [Plus AI blog](https://plusai.com/blog/how-to-stop-receiving-notifications-during-presentations); [Dispatch on Teams focus mode](https://dispatch.m.io/blog/microsoft-teams-focus-mode)
- [EXPERT] Abrahams' What / So what / Now what was presented in a virtual-meetings episode as a way to make concise contributions in virtual meetings. — [Stanford GSB Insights](https://gsb.stanford.edu/insights/quick-thinks-tips-speaking-virtual-meetings)

### Inferences
- Screen-sharing hygiene checklist (synthesized; the sources do not explicitly cover sharing a window instead of the whole screen):
  - Share a window, not the whole screen.
  - Close email and chat apps, and turn on Do Not Disturb.
  - Have the appendix open in a separate window, ready to share.
  - Test audio, camera and screen sharing beforehand.
  - Use presenter mode so your face stays visible.
  - Hide self-view if it makes you anxious (Bailenson).
- Assigning a co-host or "chat buddy" to monitor the chat and Q&A is common practice, but no authoritative source was found in this session.
- [DRAFTED] Virtual phrases:
  - "Can everyone see my screen? Just give me a thumbs-up."
  - "I'll pause every ten minutes for questions; feel free to drop them in the chat in the meantime."
  - "I see a question in the chat from Ana: she's asking about cost. Great question..."
  - "Let me stop sharing so we can see each other for the discussion."
  - "Quick poll: in the chat, type 1 if you prefer option A and 2 if you prefer option B."
  - When you freeze or drop: "Sorry, I lost connection. Where did I drop off?" / "Could you hear the last point about the budget?"
  - Bringing in a silent executive: "Carlos, I'd love your perspective on this, given your team owns the rollout."

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed evidence was found for a specific optimal virtual segment length or interaction frequency. The numbers above are practitioner and vendor claims.
- The official Microsoft Support and Zoom Support "presenting best practices" pages were not retrieved. Platform details come from Microsoft's Insider blog and a college IT page, and menu names may have changed.
- No sources were found on camera framing or eye contact (looking at the lens) beyond Bailenson.

## 6. Opening and closing: strong openings and closing on the key message (Winston)

### Takeaway
Patrick Winston (MIT, "How to Speak," 2018) recommends opening with an "empowerment promise" (what listeners will gain) and not with a joke. He recommends closing with a "Contributions" slide rather than "Conclusions" (and not "Questions?"), and ending with a "salute" rather than "thank you." Duarte's executive advice adds that, for leaders, the opening should be the conclusion itself.

### Cited Findings
- [EXPERT] Winston: "Do provide an empowerment promise" at the start, i.e., tell people what they will know by the end. "Don't start with a joke," because the audience isn't yet tuned to your voice and style. — [Cal Newport summary](https://calnewport.com/the-art-of-speaking/); [MIT OCW lecture](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-tll-005-how-to-speak-january-iap-2018/pages/how-to-speak/)
- [EXPERT] Winston: "Don't finish with a conclusion slide. Instead have a contributions slide." The final slide stays up during Q&A, so it should show what you did and delivered, not "Questions?" or "Thank you." — [Cal Newport summary](https://calnewport.com/the-art-of-speaking/); [Robin Stewart, "Winston's advice on giving talks"](https://robinstewart.com/blog/?p=132)
- [EXPERT] Winston: "Don't thank the audience," because it can suggest they did you a favor by staying. "End with a salute," e.g., "You've been a great audience..." — [Cal Newport summary](https://calnewport.com/the-art-of-speaking/)
- [EXPERT] Winston's sequence near the end: show how you delivered on the promise, then invite questions, repeating each question before answering it. — [Robin Stewart summary](https://robinstewart.com/blog/?p=132)
- [EXPERT] For executive audiences, Duarte says the opening should be the summary itself: findings, recommendation and call to action. — [Duarte blog](https://www.duarte.com/blog/how-to-effectively-present-to-senior-executives/)

### Inferences
- Winston's advice comes from academic and job-talk settings. In corporate LATAM contexts, a brief "thank you for your time" is culturally expected and harmless if it is not the last word. A practical compromise: salute or recap, take questions, then restate the key message and the decision.
- Weak openings to avoid (practitioner consensus, not formally sourced here): "Today I'm going to talk about...", apologizing for your English or for the slides, and long agendas.
- [DRAFTED] Strong openings:
  - Answer first: "We can cut onboarding time in half by Q2. Here's how, and here's what I need from you."
  - Empowerment promise: "By the end of the next ten minutes, you'll know exactly which two markets to prioritize next year and why."
  - Surprising number: "Forty percent of our tickets are the same three questions."
  - Question or stakes: "What would it mean for us if we lost Brazil's top five clients next year? Right now, three of them are at risk."
- [DRAFTED] Strong closings (instead of "Questions?"):
  - "To sum up: three things. One... two... three... And the decision we need today is X."
  - "So, back to where we started: we can cut onboarding time in half. With your approval today, we start on Monday."
  - Leave the contributions/key-message slide up during Q&A, then end with: "To close, the one thing I'd like you to take away is..."
  - Salute: "This has been a really helpful discussion. I appreciate the sharp questions." / "Great discussion, thank you all for the input on the timeline."
  - Q&A handling: "Let me repeat the question so everyone hears it: ..." / "That's a fair challenge. Here's how we thought about it."

### Gaps
