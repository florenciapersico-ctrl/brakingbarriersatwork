# Job-interview presentations: structures, templates, delivery, Q&A and follow-up

Evidence note for the report writer: almost everything found here is **practitioner or expert opinion** (recruiters, career coaches, consulting coaches, book summaries). I found **no peer-reviewed or controlled evidence** on what slide counts, structures or Q&A tactics improve hiring outcomes. Labels used below: **[Expert opinion]** = advice from a recruiter, coach or author; **[Consensus]** = the same advice from several independent practitioner sources; **[My inference]** = synthesis or wording I wrote that does not come from a source. All "ready-to-use phrases" are marked as sourced (quoted) or as my own wording.

---

## 1. 30-60-90 day plan presentations

### Takeaway
The standard spine is **Learn (1–30) → Contribute / plan and early wins (31–60) → Execute / deliver (61–90)**. Each phase has priorities, SMART goals and **one metric per goal**, tied to the job description. Because the candidate works only from public information, the plan should be framed as **hypotheses to validate**, not promises. Recruiters and coaches value "a few achievable things" over ambition. Watkins' *The First 90 Days* supplies the underlying logic: learn before acting, diagnose the situation and secure early wins.

### Cited Findings
- The Muse's step-by-step method [Expert opinion]: (1) decide the context; (2) think big picture, meaning why the company is hiring for this role; (3) ask questions to learn the **baseline** (averages, benchmarks) before setting targets; (4) meet key stakeholders within 30 days (manager, peers, cross-functional and external partners, direct reports); (5) phase focus: days 1–30 Learning, 31–60 Planning and beginning to contribute, 61–90 Execution / initiating change; (6) priorities per phase; (7) SMART goals; (8) **a metric for each goal**; (9) stay flexible and adjust on feedback — [The Muse](https://www.themuse.com/advice/30-60-90-day-plan-instructions-template-example)
- The Muse sorts goals into three types [Expert opinion]: **Learning** (knowledge and skills), **Performance** (concrete accomplishments) and **Personal** (relationships, credibility) — [The Muse](https://www.themuse.com/advice/30-60-90-day-plan-instructions-template-example)
- On metrics, The Muse advises quantifiable measures (revenue, pageviews), making qualitative goals measurable (e.g., count five-star reviews instead of citing "positive feedback"), and for sales roles tracking sales, leads and revenue — [The Muse](https://www.themuse.com/advice/30-60-90-day-plan-instructions-template-example)
- Quotes in The Muse on avoiding overpromising [Expert opinion]: Eliot Kaplan (career coach, ex-VP Talent Acquisition, Hearst): "Come up with a couple things you can accomplish successfully" and "If you come in without a game plan and try to tackle everything, you're going to get nothing done"; his pitch line: "I've got five good ideas, and when you hire me, I've got 50 more." Tamara Williams (coach): "You need to be a student before you become a teacher." The Muse also warns not to assume that a previous employer's methods will work here — [The Muse](https://www.themuse.com/advice/30-60-90-day-plan-instructions-template-example)
- For senior roles, The Muse suggests showing that you understand the department's challenges and including a few specific ideas (cutting costs, increasing sales, improving customer satisfaction) — [The Muse](https://www.themuse.com/advice/30-60-90-day-plan-instructions-template-example)
- A common interview-deck outline [Expert opinion, vendor/template site]: Introduction → First 30 Days (Learning goals) → First 60 Days (Initiative goals) → First 90 Days (Transformation goals) → **Timeline & Scorecard**. Practitioner guides suggest about 10–12 slides — [SlideModel](https://slidemodel.com/30-60-90-day-plan-job-interview/30-60-90-day-plan-for-job-interviews-cover); [LoopCV](https://blog.loopcv.pro/30-60-90-day-plan-interview/amp/)
- Inputs and constraints: candidates work from public information (the job description, company website, recent news, LinkedIn profiles of people in similar roles). Plans are usually presented in final rounds for mid-level and senior roles, and are typically 1–2 pages if delivered as a document. LoopCV advises phrasing ideas as hypotheses, e.g. "Based on what I know so far, I'd expect to focus on X; I'd validate that in the first month," and says hiring managers score "the balance of initiative and listening, not the boldness of the promises" — [LoopCV blog](https://blog.loopcv.pro/30-60-90-day-plan-interview/amp/) [Expert opinion; commercial career blog]
- Dissenting view: Ask a Manager (Alison Green) notes that some job-search advisers push unsolicited 30-60-90 plans, that only a small number of employers ask for them, and that unsolicited plans often hurt candidates because they were not done well — [Ask a Manager](https://askamanager.org/?p=9478) (via search summary; not fetched in full) [Expert opinion]
- Watkins, *The First 90 Days* (via secondary summaries; book not consulted directly):
  - The **STARS** model classifies the situation a new leader inherits: Start-up, Turnaround, Accelerated Growth (sometimes omitted in summaries), Realignment, Sustaining Success. The situation type changes what you should do first.
  - The learning phase means mapping the formal and informal organization and running a "cultural audit."
  - **Early wins** should be achievable, visible and meaningful to stakeholders.
  - Sources: [FocusU summary](https://focusu.com/blog/10-lessons-from-the-first-90-days-by-michael-d-watkins/); [ClickUp summary](https://clickup.com/blog/the-first-90-days-summary/). One summary says to identify wins by day 30 and deliver by about day 60, but that is the summary's own reading. Statistics on such sites ("50% of executives fail…") could not be verified and should **not** be used.

### Inferences
- [My inference] Template to give candidates (8–10 slides for 15 min):
  1. Title
  2. "What I understand about the role & context." Restate 3 priorities from the job description and research, labelled "my assumptions."
  3. Guiding approach (Learn → Contribute → Deliver). Optional STARS read: "This looks like a realignment / growth situation because…"
  4. Days 1–30: Learn. Stakeholder meetings, data/baseline review, listening tour. Metric: e.g. "met 15 key stakeholders; baseline KPIs documented."
  5. Days 31–60: Contribute. 1–2 quick wins drawn from the job description. Metric per win.
  6. Days 61–90: Execute. Own a larger initiative. Metric linked to role KPIs (revenue, NPS, cycle time, retention, etc.).
  7. Scorecard / timeline: one table with phase × goal × KPI × how measured.
  8. Assumptions & questions I'd validate
  9. Close: "why me" plus a question back to the panel.
- [My inference] Phrases for framing assumptions (my wording, informed by LoopCV and The Muse):
  - "Based on the job description and what I could find publicly, my working assumption is that… I'd validate this in my first few weeks."
  - "I don't have internal data yet, so please treat these numbers as placeholders. The point is the approach."
  - "In the first 30 days my priority is to listen: to understand what's working before proposing changes."
  - "One quick win I'd explore, if it's confirmed as a pain point, is…"
  - "Success at day 90, for me, would look like… measured by…"
  - "Which of these priorities matches what you're seeing internally?"
- [My inference] For non-native speakers, the hypothesis framing also lowers pressure: it invites dialogue instead of requiring a perfect monologue.

### Gaps
- I did not consult the original text of *The First 90 Days* (HBR Press). STARS labels and timing of early wins come from secondary summaries.
- I found no data on how often employers request 30-60-90 plans, or how they score them.

## 2. Case / "How would you approach X" presentations

### Takeaway
Consulting practice converges on **answer-first (Pyramid Principle)** communication framed with **SCQA** (Situation–Complication–Question–Answer). The body is a **MECE structure** and the close is a **recommendation with 2–3 supporting reasons, key risks and next steps**. Coaches agree that structure comes first. They disagree on whether to state an explicit hypothesis upfront.

### Cited Findings
- **Pyramid Principle** (Barbara Minto, developed at McKinsey; book 1985) [Expert framework]:
  - Put the governing thought or recommendation first, supported by 3–4 grouped arguments, each backed by evidence.
  - Many presenters bury the conclusion at the end.
  - SCQA sets up the answer: Situation (shared background) → Complication (change or tension) → Question → Answer.
  - Arguments should be MECE (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive).
  - Sources: [ThinkInsights](https://thinkinsights.net/strategy/pyramid-principle); [StrategyCase](https://strategycase.com/the-pyramid-principle-case-interview/)
- StrategyCase cautions that for **personal/behavioral stories** a story structure fits better than the pyramid — [StrategyCase](https://strategycase.com/the-pyramid-principle-case-interview/) [Expert opinion]
- Closing / synthesis format [Expert opinion, coaching site]: about 2–3 minutes; **lead with the recommendation**, give 2–3 data-driven reasons, then a top risk (e.g., competitor response, implementation challenge) and one specific next step (e.g., pilot, due diligence) — [Leland](https://www.joinleland.com/library/a/consulting-case-interview-guide)
- PrepLounge coaches (ex-McKinsey/BCG) [Expert opinion]:
  - **Agreement:** the standard recommendation sequence is objective → recommendation → 2–4 supporting arguments with numbers → what else to explore.
  - **Disagreement on hypotheses:**
    - One ex-McKinsey interviewer argues against stating a hypothesis before structuring, because prompts are vague.
    - Another coach says the commonest error is leading with hypotheses at the expense of structure, and that a case can be passed without stating one.
    - A BCG coach says the framework *is* a set of hypotheses and prefers natural phrasing like "my thinking here is…"
  - **Hypothesis-driven behaviour** means: explain your reasoning, always propose where to go next, and interpret numbers in context.
  - Sources: [PrepLounge – hypothesis-driven](https://www.preplounge.com/en/consulting-forum/mckinsey-hypothesis-driven-11869); [PrepLounge – when to state hypothesis](https://preplounge.com/en/consulting-forum/at-what-point-in-the-case-does-the-interviewee-state-hypothesis-9356); [PrepLounge – case structure](https://www.preplounge.com/consulting-forum/case-interview-structure-20493)
- Bain's official careers page [Official]: "Show us you can think in a logical and structured way"; case interviews "reward precision, but also creativity, and there isn't necessarily a 'right' answer." Bain provides the case materials — [Bain](https://www.bain.com/careers/interview-prep/case-interview/)
- BCG's candidate-led format: the candidate walks the interviewer through the solution end to end. McKinsey's format is more interviewer-led — [IGotAnOffer](https://igotanoffer.com/pages/bcg-case-interview) [Expert opinion, secondary]
- Michael Page [Expert opinion]: build around **2–3 key points**; every slide should advance them; use a compelling introduction, data-backed arguments and a firm conclusion — [Michael Page](https://www.michaelpage.com/advice/career-advice/job-interview-tips/how-sell-yourself-interview-presentation)

### Inferences
- [My inference] Template for a "How would you approach X" presentation (non-consulting roles, 15–20 min):
  1. Title, with the **answer as the title**, e.g. "Recommendation: Focus 2027 growth on existing enterprise accounts"
  2. Agenda
  3. My understanding of the problem (Situation + Complication), including what I assumed
  4. The question we're answering, and success criteria / KPIs
  5. Options considered (2–3), with simple criteria (impact, cost, time, risk)
  6. Recommendation + 3 reasons
  7. Implementation roadmap (phases / first 90 days)
  8. Risks & mitigations
  9. Next steps / what I'd validate
  10. Close
- [My inference] Phrases (my wording):
  - "Let me start with my recommendation, and then walk you through how I got there."
  - "The situation, as I understand it, is… The challenge is… So the key question is…"
  - "I looked at three options. I'll compare them on impact, cost and time to deliver."
  - "My recommendation is X, for three reasons. First… Second… Third…"
  - "The main risk is… and I would mitigate it by…"
  - "If I had more data, the first thing I'd want to test is…"
  - "My thinking here is…" (BCG coach's suggested natural phrasing)

## 3. "Tell us about a project / your achievements" presentations

### Takeaway
Adapt **STAR (Situation–Task–Action–Result)** or **CAR (Challenge–Action–Result)** into about 4–6 slides. Keep the setup short. Spend the most time on **your own actions** (say "I," not just "we") and nearly as much on **quantified results**. Add a learnings slide and a "relevance to this role" bridge.

### Cited Findings
- STAR turns a long, wandering answer into a focused story and works for presenting projects and case studies. For behavioral answers: be specific about the situation; spend most time on actions; use "I" rather than "we"; quantify results (%, money, time saved); "increased sales by 25%" beats "improved sales"; spend nearly as much time on results as on actions. Common pitfalls are an overlong setup, a vague result and an unclear personal role — [Cake](https://www.cake.me/resources/interview-guide/star-interview-method); [Introverted Recruiter](https://theintrovertedrecruiter.substack.com/p/ace-your-next-interview-with-the-86b); [JobStreet](https://ph.jobstreet.com/career-advice/article/examples-using-star-method-job-interview) [Expert opinion]
- Indeed suggests that **each section** of an interview presentation should state its relevance, give evidence or examples, and **explain how it relates to the role** — [Indeed](https://in.indeed.com/career-advice/interviewing/interview-presentation) [Expert opinion]
- Hays: align content with the skills listed in the job description to prove you can do the job — [Hays](https://www.hays.co.uk/career-advice/article/four-tips-for-your-interview-presentation) [Expert opinion]
- Michael Page: include examples that demonstrate your hard and soft skills; keep data "rock-solid" and cite sources — [Michael Page](https://www.michaelpage.com/advice/career-advice/job-interview-tips/how-sell-yourself-interview-presentation) [Expert opinion]

### Inferences
- [My inference] Slide template (10 min, about 6–8 slides):
  1. Title: an outcome headline, e.g. "Cutting onboarding time by 40% across 3 countries"
  2. Context (Situation/Challenge) in one slide
  3. My goal / task and success measure
  4. What I did (Actions), 1–2 slides with 3 key decisions
  5. Results, with 1–3 headline numbers and a before/after visual
  6. What I learned / would do differently
  7. **How this applies to your team**: bridge to the job description and its KPIs
  8. Questions
- [My inference] Phrases (my wording):
  - "I'd like to walk you through a project that I think is directly relevant to this role."
  - "The challenge was…" / "My specific role was…"
  - "I made three key decisions. The first was…"
  - "As a result, we reduced X by 30% in six months, which saved around €200K a year."
  - "Looking back, one thing I'd do differently is…"
  - "The reason I chose this example is that your team is facing a similar challenge with…"
- [My inference] Respect confidentiality: anonymize the client or former employer's data and use ranges or percentages where needed. This matches the general advice to remove confidential information before sharing decks.

### Gaps
- No source specifically on CAR vs STAR for slide decks. The slide templates above are my own adaptation.

## 4. Practical logistics: slides, timing, agenda/closing, handouts, advance sending, tech backup, virtual

### Takeaway
**[Consensus]**
- Clarify the brief first.
- Aim for roughly one slide per minute at most: about 7–8 for 10 minutes. Many coaches prefer fewer.
- Build in an overview/agenda at the start and a summary at the end.
- Keep slides sparse and leave time for Q&A.
- Bring backups: PDF, USB, email to yourself and the recruiter, and printed copies.
- Online, share only the window and test beforehand.

### Cited Findings
- **Clarify the brief** (Michael Page): ask about topics, length, preferred tools/data, equipment, whether questions come during or after, and how much the panel knows. Pitch high-level for managers and board members, more detail for specialists. Asking about a vague brief "shows you don't just follow instructions blindly" — [Michael Page](https://www.michaelpage.com/advice/career-advice/job-interview-tips/how-sell-yourself-interview-presentation)
- The Muse: ask about length (it may be 10 minutes instead of 40), skills to showcase, number of attendees and IT setup. Keep to time: running over means being cut off or losing time for your other skills. Announce the structure at the start. Use tools you already know rather than learning Prezi. If you lose your place, pause, take a sip of water and check your notes — [The Muse](https://www.themuse.com/advice/7-tips-to-help-you-nail-that-interview-presentation)
- **Slide count:** Robert Walters suggests 4–5 slides for 5 minutes and 7–8 for 10 minutes (via search snippet; page fetch blocked). Others use "1 slide per minute." Hays and Michael Page give no number — [Robert Walters](https://www.robertwalters.co.uk/insights/career-advice/blog/top-tips-to-ace-an-interview-presentation.html); [SlidesAI](https://www.slidesai.io/blog/interview-presentation)
- **Slide structure** (Hays): first slide = overview of contents; then an intro with an attention-grabbing fact, quote or statistic; each slide gets a headline plus one visual or a few bullets; "A couple of lines per page is ample"; avoid jargon; finish by summarising key messages; leave time for questions; match the company's fonts and colours; proofread — [Hays](https://www.hays.co.uk/career-advice/article/four-tips-for-your-interview-presentation)
- **Title / agenda** (Indeed): title slide with your name, the position and a short title/subtitle; intro with goals and a brief personal intro; agenda; background; distinct sections — [Indeed](https://in.indeed.com/career-advice/interviewing/interview-presentation)
- Michael Page: "Each slide should be like a road sign"; "less is more"; the presentation should work without the visuals; put detailed studies and data in an **appendix / handout** — [Michael Page](https://www.michaelpage.com/advice/career-advice/job-interview-tips/how-sell-yourself-interview-presentation)
- **Backup:** Hays recommends a USB copy, emailing the deck to yourself and to the recruiter (who can pass it on), **printed copies for every interviewer**, and checking venue tech in advance. Michael Page: "Don't rely solely on the technology"; bring handwritten notes or flash cards. The Muse: bring your laptop and charger, a PDF copy and printed copies — [Hays](https://www.hays.co.uk/career-advice/article/four-tips-for-your-interview-presentation); [Michael Page](https://www.michaelpage.com/advice/career-advice/job-interview-tips/how-sell-yourself-interview-presentation); [The Muse](https://www.themuse.com/advice/7-tips-to-help-you-nail-that-interview-presentation)
- **Rehearsal:** practise in front of someone, or record yourself on your phone; time every run — [Michael Page](https://www.michaelpage.com/advice/career-advice/job-interview-tips/how-sell-yourself-interview-presentation); [Hays](https://www.hays.co.uk/career-advice/article/four-tips-for-your-interview-presentation)
- **Virtual (Zoom/Teams)**, from university and event guidance, not recruiter-specific [Expert opinion]:
  - Audio is the most important element.
  - Use 16:9 slides.
  - Share the specific window or app, not the whole desktop.
  - Close apps and turn off notifications.
  - Open the deck before the call.
  - Test by joining from a second device to see the attendee view.
  - **Email slides to the host ahead of time (e.g., the day before) so they can present if your screen-share fails.**
  - Sources: [VCBA guidance PDF](https://www.vcba.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/092525-EPP-Bloom-Materials.pdf); [Trinity College](https://edtech.domains.trincoll.edu/?p=8614); [Monash FLEET guide](https://archive.fleet.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/how-to-zoom.pdf); [Glassdoor Zoom tips](https://www.glassdoor.co.uk/blog/zoom-interview/)
- **Sending slides in advance:**
  - I found no recruiter guidance that says definitively to send or not send slides before the interview.
  - Hays suggests sending a copy to the recruiter as a backup. Virtual guidance suggests sending to the host the day before.
  - Indeed and The Muse do not address it. Best practice is to ask.

### Inferences
- [My inference] Slide budget, derived from "≤1 slide/min, leave time for Q&A":
  - **10 min:** about 6–8 content slides
  - **15 min:** about 8–12
  - **20 min:** about 10–15
  - Plan to finish 10–15% early, and always add a title slide and a closing/questions slide.
- [My inference] Phrases for clarifying the brief (email to the recruiter):
  - "Could you confirm how long the presentation should be, and whether questions will be during or after?"
  - "Who will be on the panel, and what are their roles?"
  - "Would you like me to send the slides in advance, and in which format?"
  - "Will I be presenting in person or via Teams/Zoom, and will I be able to share my screen?"
- [My inference] Phrases for the opening, agenda and close:
  - "Thank you for having me. In the next 15 minutes I'll cover three things: …, … and … I'll leave about five minutes at the end for your questions, but feel free to interrupt."
  - "To sum up, the three things I'd like you to remember are…"
  - "That brings me to the end. I'd really welcome your questions and feedback."
- [My inference] Phrases for tech failure:
  - "It looks like my screen share isn't working. I sent the deck to [name] yesterday; would you mind sharing it from your side while I talk you through it?"
  - "No problem, I have printed copies here."
- [My inference] For non-native speakers:
  - Put the key number or message in the slide headline, so the slide carries the meaning if pronunciation wobbles.
  - Write speaker notes as short prompts, not a script. This matches Hays' advice not to read from a script.

### Gaps
- I found no empirical study on the optimal slide count for interview presentations. All numbers are rules of thumb.
- The Robert Walters and Glassdoor pages were blocked (403), so slide counts come from search snippets.

## 5. Q&A: anticipating questions, pushback, "I don't know", bridging, ending strongly

### Takeaway
**[Consensus, practitioner]**
- Pre-list the toughest questions.
- Restate or clarify a question before answering, and pause.
- Never bluff. If you don't know, say so, reason aloud or bridge to related experience, and offer to follow up (then actually follow up in the thank-you email).
- Treat pushback calmly: separate the substance from the tone.
- End with a summary of key messages.

### Cited Findings
- **Anticipate:** list the questions you hope no one asks and prepare answers. Group them into three: answer confidently / need more data / address offline — [The Visual Communication Guy](https://thevisualcommunicationguy.com/2024/09/19/handling-tough-questions-during-executive-presentations-essential-strategies-for-success/); [University of Guelph – ABCs of Answering Difficult Questions](https://learningcommons.lib.uoguelph.ca/static/953dab30d35c771c4c5a22f3eb92a75f/ABCs%20of%20Answering%20Difficult%20Questions.pdf) [Expert opinion]
- **Restate / pause:** restating the question confirms understanding, buys time and lets everyone hear it. Pausing is not a sign of uncertainty — same sources [Expert opinion]
- **Hostile questions:** don't raise your tone or get defensive; separate valid criticism from tone; respond to the substance; offer to discuss privately if needed. One source advises against "That's a good question" because it can make others feel their questions were weaker — same sources [Expert opinion; note that The Muse below *uses* "that's a great question", so sources conflict on this phrase]
- **The Muse's 4 ways for questions you can't answer** [Expert opinion]:
  1. **Take your time:** "Hmm…that's a great question. Let me think about that."
  2. **Think aloud:** structure with "first," "then," "lastly," and qualify ("the process varies depending on the situation").
  3. **Redirect** to related experience.
  4. **Fail-safe:** "That's not a concept I'm really familiar with yet," followed by enthusiasm and what you do know.
  - Source: [The Muse](https://www.themuse.com/advice/4-ways-to-handle-interview-questions-you-dont-know-how-to-answer)
- **Don't bluff:** if someone in the room knows the answer, you lose credibility. Instead of a bare "I don't know," offer to follow up, and you can answer it in the follow-up email — [Hiration](https://www.hiration.com/blog/how-to-answer-interview-questions-you-dont-know/); [SkillsYouNeed](https://www.skillsyouneed.com/rhubarb/difficult-interview-questions.html) [Expert opinion]
- **Ending:** finish by summarising the key messages you want remembered — [Hays](https://www.hays.co.uk/career-advice/article/four-tips-for-your-interview-presentation)
- Ask in advance whether questions come during or after — [Michael Page](https://www.michaelpage.com/advice/career-advice/job-interview-tips/how-sell-yourself-interview-presentation)

### Inferences
- [My inference] Ready-to-use phrases (my wording unless quoted):
  - **Clarify / buy time** (also useful when a non-native speaker didn't catch the question):
    - "Just to make sure I understand: you're asking whether…?"
    - "Could you say a bit more about what you mean by…?"
    - "Sorry, could you repeat the last part? I want to make sure I answer the right question."
    - "Let me think about that for a second."
  - **Pushback:**
    - "That's a fair challenge. You're right that… The reason I still recommend X is…"
    - "I see it differently, and here's why…"
    - "You may well have information I don't have. If that's the case, I'd adjust the plan by…"
    - "What would make this more realistic from your perspective?"
  - **Don't know:**
    - "I don't know the exact figure, but my estimate would be… based on…"
    - "I haven't worked with that tool directly, but I've used [similar] to…"
    - "I'd rather not guess. Can I come back to you on that by email tomorrow?"
  - **Bridging:**
    - "That connects to a point I made earlier about…"
    - "What I can tell you is…"
    - "The bigger question there, I think, is…"
  - **Closing strongly:**
    - "If there's one thing I'd like you to take away, it's…"
    - "To bring it back to the role: this is exactly the kind of challenge I'd be excited to own here."
    - "Before we finish, may I ask: is there anything in what I presented that you'd like me to expand on, or any concerns I can address?"
- [My inference] Rehearse the Q&A as heavily as the talk. The pre-listed questions technique pairs well with the AT WORK role-play practice.

## 6. Follow-up after the presentation

### Takeaway
Send a short thank-you email within about 24 hours. Reference a specific moment or question from the presentation. Attach the deck as a PDF if appropriate or requested. Use the email to answer any question you couldn't answer on the spot, and reaffirm interest.

### Cited Findings
- Send within 24 hours, focusing on appreciation and one or two interview highlights. Other sources say 1–2 days — [NerdWallet](https://www.nerdwallet.com/article/finance/examples-of-thank-you-emails-to-send-after-an-interview); [Big Interview](https://resources.biginterview.com/interview-thank-you-email/) [Expert opinion]
- Answering an unanswered question in the follow-up email shows persistence — [Hiration](https://www.hiration.com/blog/how-to-answer-interview-questions-you-dont-know/) [Expert opinion]
- Some guides suggest including slides or ideas in follow-ups, especially for business development and sales roles — [Maestro Labs](https://maestrolabs.com/how-to/thank-you-email-after-interview) (via search summary) [Expert opinion]
- Hays suggests sending the deck to the recruiter as a backup before the interview, so the recruiter channel is a natural route — [Hays](https://www.hays.co.uk/career-advice/article/four-tips-for-your-interview-presentation)

### Inferences
- [My inference] Email template (my wording):
  > **Subject:** Thank you – [Role] presentation | [Your name]
  >
