# Delivery, anxiety management and rehearsal for presentations in a second language (focus: Spanish-speaking professional women presenting in English)

Evidence labels used throughout:
- **[EVIDENCE]**: peer-reviewed study or experiment
- **[EXPERT]**: expert or practitioner opinion, teaching framework, or guide
- **[WEAK/CONTESTED]**: small, preliminary, not replicated, or disputed

Note on sourcing: most findings come from abstracts, publisher pages and university pages found through search. The full text was checked only for Balban et al. 2023. Exact numbers that came from secondary summaries are flagged.

---

## 1. Pace, pausing and thought groups: what research says about speech rate and comprehension

### Takeaway
Speaking rate is one of the strongest predictors of how easy L2 speech is to understand (comprehensibility). The relationship is curvilinear: very slow and very fast speech both score worse. Listeners prefer L2 speech that is somewhat *faster* than L2 speakers typically produce, so the advice is "don't rush, but don't crawl." Too many or too-long pauses hurt, while pauses placed at thought-group boundaries help listeners. No reliable universal "ideal wpm" for non-native speakers exists.

### Cited Findings
- **[EVIDENCE]** Munro & Derwing (2001, *Studies in Second Language Acquisition* 23, 451–468): there was a significant curvilinear relationship between speaking rate and accentedness and comprehensibility ratings. In a second experiment, rate was manipulated with compression/expansion software, which showed the effect came from rate itself and not from proficiency. Listeners gave the best ratings to speech somewhat *faster* than L2 speakers' typical rate. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/modeling-perceptions-of-the-accentedness-and-comprehensibility-of-l2-speech-the-role-of-speaking-rate/E22C75E244694BF6D6E083149FBEFA51); [Munro's publication list, SFU](https://www.sfu.ca/~mjmunro/articles-and-chapters.html)
  - Partially conflicting: Trofimovich & Baker (2006) found faster speech was rated *less* accented, a linear rather than curvilinear pattern. Very few studies examine rate and *intelligibility* (as opposed to ratings). — summarized in [Isaacs (UCL)](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1525195/1/Isaacs_AAM_13311.pdf)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Kang (2010, *System*): Kang acoustically analyzed 5-minute lecture samples from 11 international teaching assistants, which 58 US undergraduates rated. Comprehensibility was mostly associated with *speaking rate*. Accentedness was best predicted by *pitch range and word stress*. Each suprasegmental feature contributed independently. — [NAU Experts](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/relative-salience-of-suprasegmental-features-on-judgments-of-l2-c/)
  - A secondary review (Gordon & Darcy 2016) summarizes Kang as showing that too many or too-long pauses, and speaking too slowly or too fast, hurt comprehensibility. This claim was not confirmed in the abstract. — [Gordon & Darcy 2016, JSLP (PDF)](https://psycholinguistics.indiana.edu/papers/GordonDarcy(2016)JSLP.pdf)
- **[EVIDENCE, synthetic speech]** A 2007 *Computer Speech & Language* study with 80 non-native and 80 native listeners: synthetic speech at 178 wpm reduced comprehension compared with 155 wpm for both groups. Persuasiveness did not change. Noise was also manipulated. — [Western Sydney University](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/synthesized-speech-intelligibility-and-persuasion-speech-rate-and/)
- **[EVIDENCE, weak/old]** A 1979 study of ESL listeners (n=20) heard time-altered recordings at 100–300 wpm. The most *preferred* rate was ~200 wpm, and 100 and 300 wpm were least preferred. This measured preference, not comprehension. — [CJSLPA 1979 (PDF)](https://cjslpa.ca/files/1979_HumComm_Vol_04/No_02_253-297/Leeper_Mashunkashey_HumComm_1979.pdf)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Non-native listeners perceive the same speech as *faster* than native listeners do. This effect is smaller in more proficient learners and correlates negatively with comprehension (review, Frontiers in Psychology 2017). — [Frontiers (PDF)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01063/pdf)
- **[EXPERT, pedagogical]** Judy Gilbert's "Prosody Pyramid" (Cambridge, 2008) has four levels: thought group → focus word → stressed syllable → peak vowel. A thought group is a chunk carrying one idea and is usually separated by a short pause. Each thought group has one focus word, usually the last content word. Gilbert says the elements are interdependent and should be taught through template sentences. — [Gilbert, Teaching Pronunciation Using the Prosody Pyramid (Cambridge PDF)](https://www.cambridge.org/us/esl/clearspeech/TeachingPronunBklt.pdf); [Baruch CUNY Tools for Clear Speech – Thought Groups](https://tfcs.baruch.cuny.edu/thought-groups/); [CJAL review](https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/CJAL/article/download/19934/21808/26517)
- **[EXPERT]** Gilbert frames English pronunciation as learning "the specifically English way of making a speaker's thoughts easy to follow." — [Cambridge PDF](https://www.cambridge.org/us/esl/clearspeech/TeachingPronunBklt.pdf)
- **[EXPERT]** Julian Treasure (TEDGlobal 2013, "How to speak so that people want to listen") lists vocal tools including register, timbre, prosody, pace (including silence), pitch and volume. Secondary summaries disagree on the exact list. He ends with vocal warm-ups. — [Singju Post transcript](https://singjupost.com/how-to-speak-so-that-people-want-to-listen-by-julian-treasure-transcript/); [Speeko summary](https://www.speeko.co/blog/summary-ted-talk-julian-treasure-how-to-speak); [Prezi outline](https://prezi.com/27zwow3yb2wg/how-to-speak-so-that-people-want-to-listen/)

### Inferences
- The coaching message should be "slow down" only for speakers who are rushing. Many L2 speakers are already slower than optimal, and making them slower can *lower* comprehensibility. A better instruction is "chunk and pause at boundaries, keep a natural pace inside the chunk."
- Non-native listeners hear speech as faster, so the pace that works for an international audience is likely slower than for a native-only audience. This is an inference: no study gives a wpm threshold.
- Pausing *between* thought groups (planned) and hesitation pausing *inside* phrases (unplanned) probably affect listeners differently. Marking scripts with "/" at thought-group boundaries puts pauses in the useful places.

### Gaps
- No reliable source gives an evidence-based "ideal wpm" for L2 presenters. Figures like "130–150 wpm" are common in coaching content, but I found no peer-reviewed basis for them.
- The exact syllables-per-second values in Munro & Derwing 2001 were not retrieved.
- No study was found that directly tests the "/ and //" script-marking technique as an intervention. It is a standard pedagogical practice (Gilbert-style) but has not been tested on its own.

## 2. Intelligibility vs accent (Munro & Derwing), and what matters most to teach

### Takeaway
Accentedness, comprehensibility and intelligibility are related but partially independent. A strong accent can be fully intelligible. The research-backed goal is being understood with little effort, not sounding native. Prosody/suprasegmentals (stress, rhythm, rate, pitch) have an outsized effect on comprehensibility.

### Cited Findings
- **[EVIDENCE]** Munro & Derwing (1995, *Language Learning* 45(1), 73–97) defined three constructs:
  - **Intelligibility**: how much is actually understood, measured by transcription.
  - **Comprehensibility**: perceived ease or effort of understanding, measured by rating.
  - **Accentedness**: how different the speech sounds from the listener's variety.

  Speech "can be heavily accented but highly intelligible." — [FORRT replication atlas entry](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1111/j.1467-1770.1995.tb00963.x/); [Benjamins: "Foreign accent, comprehensibility and intelligibility, redux"](https://www.benjamins.com/catalog/bct.121.02mun)
- **[EVIDENCE]** 25 years of follow-up research has repeatedly shown that the three dimensions are partially independent. This was revisited in a 2020 *Journal of Second Language Pronunciation* special issue. — [Benjamins JSLP 2020](https://benjamins.com/catalog/jslp.20038.mun); [Benjamins book edition](https://www.benjamins.com/catalog/bct.121.02mun)
- **[EVIDENCE]** In a separate 1995 study, Munro & Derwing found that Mandarin-accented sentences took longer to verify than native ones. Degree of accent did not predict processing time. — [Sonicfield library summary](https://sonicfield.org/library/processing-time-accent-and-comprehensibility-in-the-perception-of-native-and-for)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Derwing, Munro & Wiebe (1998, *Language Learning* 48(3), 393–410) is widely cited as evidence for prosody-focused ("broad framework") instruction improving comprehensibility. Gordon, Darcy & Ewert (2012) found that only the group given *explicit suprasegmental* instruction improved comprehensibility significantly from pre- to post-test. — [Iowa State PSLLT proceedings](https://iastatedigitalpress.com/psllt/article/id/15215). Note: the 1998 abstract itself was not retrieved.
- **[EVIDENCE]** Kang (2010): accent ratings were best predicted by pitch range and word stress, and comprehensibility by speaking rate. — [NAU Experts](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/relative-salience-of-suprasegmental-features-on-judgments-of-l2-c/)

### Spanish-speaker-specific issues
- **[EVIDENCE]** Spanish speakers find *vowel reduction* (unstressed vowels → schwa) harder than *stress placement* (Flege & Bohn, instrumental study, *SSLA*). — [Cambridge](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/an-instrumental-study-of-vowel-reduction-and-stress-placement-in-spanishaccented-english/EEEE9B0B559EFA114C100452428B0228)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Byers & Yavas (2017, PLOS ONE): for bilinguals whose L1 lacks large stressed/unstressed vowel contrasts (Spanish), reducing unstressed vowels to schwa is problematic. — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5383264/)
- **[EVIDENCE, thesis]** Spanish speakers insert a vowel before initial s-clusters ("e-school", "e-speak"). — [Universidad de Valladolid TFG (PDF)](https://uvadoc.uva.es/bitstream/handle/10324/33151/TFG_F_2018_13.pdf?sequence=1)
- **[EXPERT]** Other issues reported in teaching sources:
  - Spanish rhythm is more even (syllable-timed), which creates an accent even when individual sounds are correct.
  - Spanish rarely ends words in consonant clusters, so speakers drop final consonants. This affects -ed and plural -s and can blur grammar.
  - The English vowel inventory gets mapped onto 5 Spanish vowels (ship/sheep).
  - Speakers match cognates to the Spanish syllable count, e.g. "advanced" pronounced with 3 syllables.

  — [TEFL Academy](https://www.theteflacademy.com/blog/top-pronunciation-challenges-for-spanish-speaking-english-learners-and-how-to-fix-them/); [tefl.net](https://www.tefl.net/elt/articles/teacher-technique/spanish-speaker-pronunciation-problems/); [Connected Speech Pathology](https://connectedspeechpathology.com/blog/how-to-reduce-your-spanish-accent)

### Inferences
- For Spanish speakers, the highest-impact targets are probably:
  1. Stress *timing*: reduce unstressed syllables and lengthen the stressed one. This matters more than learning *where* stress falls.
  2. Focus words / sentence stress.
  3. Final consonants that carry grammar (-ed, -s, -t/-d).
  4. No epenthetic "e-" before s-clusters.

  This prioritization combines the prosody-matters evidence with the Spanish-specific findings.
- Cognates (e.g. "development", "comfortable", "company") are high-risk stress errors, because learners transfer the Spanish stress pattern and syllable count. Coaches should pre-mark stress on cognates in presentation scripts. This is an inference from the expert sources.
- Framing for the client: "accent reduction" is not the goal. Research supports aiming for intelligibility and comprehensibility, which can also reduce anxiety about accent.

### Gaps
- I found no study isolating which single feature most improves comprehensibility for *Spanish* L1 speakers in presentation contexts.
- The claim that roughly half of Spanish speakers drop final /d/ comes from a single teacher's estimate and should not be used as a statistic.

## 3. Managing speaking anxiety: reappraisal, breathing, distanced self-talk, exposure, foreign language anxiety, power posing

### Takeaway
The best-supported quick tools are:
- Reappraising arousal as excitement ("I am excited"), from Brooks 2014.
- Distanced self-talk using your own name or "you", from Kross 2014.
- An exhale-emphasized breath such as cyclic sighing. Its evidence is modest, comes from a small study, and shows mood gains but no edge over mindfulness on anxiety.

Repeated exposure (rehearsal in front of real people) is the strongest long-term treatment. Foreign language anxiety is a distinct, well-measured construct centered on speaking. Power posing affects *felt* power at most. It should not be sold as changing hormones or performance.

### Cited Findings
- **[EVIDENCE]** Brooks (2014, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 143(3), 1144–1158), "Get Excited":
  - Across karaoke, public speaking and math tasks, people who reappraised anxiety as excitement felt more excited and performed better than people who tried to calm down.
  - Minimal interventions worked, such as saying "I am excited" aloud.
  - In the speech study, the excited group spoke longer and was rated more persuasive, competent and confident.
  - Mechanism: arousal congruency. Anxiety and excitement are both high-arousal, so only valence needs to change.
  - ~85% of surveyed people believed "calm down" was the best advice.

  — [PubMed 24364682](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24364682/); [APA full text PDF](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/xge-a0035325.pdf)
- **[EVIDENCE, moderate/small]** Balban et al. (2023, *Cell Reports Medicine* 4(1):100895), Stanford (Spiegel & Huberman labs). This was a remote RCT of 5 min/day for 28 days with 108 participants, ~30 of them in cyclic sighing.
  - **Cyclic sighing protocol**: a slow inhale, then a second shorter inhale to fill the lungs, then a long slow exhale. Ideally nose in, mouth out.
  - Cyclic sighing increased positive affect more than mindfulness and reduced respiratory rate more.
  - **State anxiety fell similarly in all groups**, with no significant difference vs mindfulness. HRV and resting heart rate did not change.
  - Authors' limitations: exploratory, retrospectively registered, remote, self-reported adherence, small groups, mostly one university class, 4-week follow-up only.

  — [PMC9873947 full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9873947); [Huberman Lab publication page](https://hubermanlab.stanford.edu/publications/brief-structured-respiration-practices-enhance-mood-and-reduce-physiological-arousal?.com)
  - **[WEAK/CONTESTED]** Popular claims that a *single* "physiological sigh" instantly calms you right before a talk go beyond this study, which tested 5 minutes daily over a month.
- **[EVIDENCE]** Kross et al. (2014, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*), distanced self-talk:
  - Seven studies, reported as 585 participants (figure from secondary source).
  - Before a stressful job-qualification speech with 5 minutes of preparation, people who used their own name or "you" instead of "I" reported less distress and were rated as performing better.
  - Effect sizes were described as modest. The sample was mostly undergraduates.

  — [University of Michigan news](https://prod.lsa.umich.edu/psych/news-events/all-news/archived-news/2014/02/talking-in-the-3rd-person-lowers-anxiety--study.html); [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/articles/201505/the-voice-reason)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Horwitz, Horwitz & Cope (1986, *Modern Language Journal* 70(2), 125–132):
  - Defined Foreign Language (Classroom) Anxiety as a distinct anxiety. Its three components are communication apprehension, test anxiety and fear of negative evaluation.
  - Created the 33-item FLCAS (reported α ≈ .93, test-retest .83 over 8 weeks).
  - Later research finds this anxiety arises predominantly in *speaking* situations.

  — [Horwitz et al. 1986 (PDF, OSU)](https://www.ling.ohio-state.edu/research/groups/lacqueys/readings/horowitz.pdf); [HRMARS FLCAS paper](https://hrmars.com/papers_submitted/14534/foreign-language-classroom-anxiety-scale-flcas-working-title-what-causes-foreign-language-anxiety.pdf)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Exposure for public speaking anxiety: a meta-analysis (Behavior Modification) found large effects for both in-vivo and virtual-reality exposure vs control, with in-vivo marginally better. — [Queen's University Belfast thesis record](https://pure.qub.ac.uk/en/studentTheses/virtual-reality-exposure-therapy-as-a-psychological-intervention-/); [NYU IFP summary](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2021/meta-analyses-systematic-reviews/0145445521991102/)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Video feedback combined with exposure (UT Austin, Telch lab, placebo-controlled RCT):
  - Contrasting one's *imagined* performance with the *actual recorded* performance sped up anxiety reduction.
  - The effect was cognitively mediated: people realized they looked less anxious than they felt.
  - At 1-month follow-up there were no differences between exposure conditions.

  — [Telch lab PDF](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/telch/files/2015/02/The-efﬁcacy-of-videotape-feedback.pdf); [UT repository](https://repositories.lib.utexas.edu/handle/2152/2211?show=full)
- **[EVIDENCE, older]** A meta-analysis of 12 classroom studies (1998) found videotaped feedback improved speech content and skills with no significant increase in anxiety. — [Missouri State BearWorks](https://bearworks.missouristate.edu/articles-coal/349/)
- **[WEAK/CONTESTED]** Power posing:
  - Ranehill et al. (2015, n=200) replicated the effect on *feelings* of power but found no effect on hormones or behavior.
  - Simmons & Simonsohn's (2017) p-curve of 33 studies was consistent with zero effect plus selective reporting.
  - Gronau et al. (2017) found strong evidence for an effect on *felt* power.
  - Cuddy, Schultz & Fosse (2018) argue there is support for effects on feelings, emotions and self-evaluations.
  - The dispute is ongoing. Original co-author Carney publicly distanced herself, though that statement was not verified here.

  — [Simmons & Simonsohn 2017 (PDF)](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/Simmons-Simonsohn-2017.pdf); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2791272); [UPenn repository](https://repository.upenn.edu/fnce_papers/76); [Columbia stat modeling blog on "17 replications" claim](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/2018/04/20/carol-nickerson-investigates-unfounded-claim-17-replications/)
- **[EXPERT]** Matt Abrahams (Stanford GSB, *Speaking Up Without Freaking Out*, *Think Faster, Talk Smarter*):
  - Treat anxiety as normal and evolutionary; first calm the body.
  - A secondary summary attributes to him a deep belly breath with the exhale about 2× the inhale.
  - Aim for "good enough" rather than perfect, to free cognitive bandwidth.
  - Use structures such as "What? So what? Now what?"

  — [Upworthy summary](https://www.upworthy.com/6-word-framework-stanford-professor-matt-abrahams/); [Antoine Buteau notes](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-matt-abrahams/); [getAbstract](https://journal.getabstract.com/en/?p=126815)

### Inferences
- A suggested pre-talk sequence assembled from separate studies (the combination itself is untested):
  1. 1–2 minutes of slow, exhale-long breathing to bring breathing rate down.
  2. Say "I'm excited" aloud.
  3. Use distanced self-talk ("Florencia, you know this material").
- For L2 speakers, part of the anxiety is fear of negative evaluation of their *English*, which is Horwitz's construct. The intelligibility research (accent ≠ unintelligible) can be used as a cognitive reframe.
- Recording and reviewing practice runs may reduce anxiety by correcting the belief that "I looked as nervous as I felt." The effect seems to speed up improvement rather than change the long-term outcome.
- Power posing should be offered only as "may help you *feel* more confident," never as a hormonal or performance effect.

### Gaps
- No study found tests "I'm excited" reappraisal specifically with L2 speakers or in a foreign language.
- No study found tests a single pre-talk physiological sigh on speaking performance.
- Large-scale replication status of Brooks 2014 and Kross 2014 was not verified in this search.

## 4. Rehearsal methods: aloud vs silent, recording, Q&A, openings/closings, notes

### Takeaway
Rehearsing *aloud* beats silent rehearsal for remembering content (production effect). Hearing a recording of yourself also helps. Video review improves skills and can speed up anxiety reduction. Exposure in front of real people is the most effective anxiety treatment. Advice on memorizing openings and closings and on keyword notes is expert practice, not experimentally tested.

### Cited Findings
- **[EVIDENCE]** Production effect (Forrin & MacLeod 2017, *Memory*; University of Waterloo, n=95):
  - Four ways of learning were compared: reading silently, hearing another person read, hearing a recording of oneself, and reading aloud.
  - Reading aloud gave the best memory and silent reading the worst.
  - Hearing one's *own* recorded voice beat hearing another person.

  — [University of Waterloo news](https://uwaterloo.ca/news/news/study-finds-reading-information-aloud-yourself-improves); [MacLeod lab PDF (production effect, JEP:LMC 2010)](https://uwaterloo.ca/memory-attention-cognition-lab/sites/default/files/uploads/files/jeplmc10-2.pdf)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Video feedback improves public-speaking skills without raising anxiety (1998 meta-analysis of 12 studies). Comparing imagined with actual performance speeds anxiety reduction. — [Missouri State BearWorks](https://bearworks.missouristate.edu/articles-coal/349/); [Telch lab PDF](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/telch/files/2015/02/The-efﬁcacy-of-videotape-feedback.pdf)
- **[EVIDENCE]** In-vivo exposure (in front of people) is marginally better than VR exposure. Both have large effects on public speaking anxiety. — [QUB thesis record](https://pure.qub.ac.uk/en/studentTheses/virtual-reality-exposure-therapy-as-a-psychological-intervention-/)
- **[EXPERT]** Abrahams: use a structure to cut cognitive load in spontaneous speaking (e.g. Q&A): "What? So what? Now what?" Aim for connection over perfection. — [Upworthy](https://www.upworthy.com/6-word-framework-stanford-professor-matt-abrahams/); [Antoine Buteau notes](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-matt-abrahams/)
- **[EXPERT]** Recording yourself and comparing it with a model is widely recommended for Spanish speakers' pronunciation practice. — [Pronounce.tv blog](https://pronounce.tv/blog/common-spanish-speaker-pronunciation-mistakes); [TEFL Academy](https://www.theteflacademy.com/blog/top-pronunciation-challenges-for-spanish-speaking-english-learners-and-how-to-fix-them/)
- **[EXPERT]** Treasure recommends vocal warm-ups before speaking. — [Singju Post transcript](https://singjupost.com/how-to-speak-so-that-people-want-to-listen-by-julian-treasure-transcript/)

### Inferences: practical routines a coach can assign
These are built from the evidence above. The routines themselves have not been tested as packages.

1. **Script marking (Gilbert-based):**
   - Mark "/" at thought-group boundaries (short pause) and "//" at idea or section ends (longer pause).
   - Underline the focus word in each thought group.
   - Put a stress mark (ˈ) on the stressed syllable of every multi-syllable word, prioritizing cognates (deˈvelopment, ˈcomfortable, ˈcompany, ecoˈnomic) and key numbers.
   - Circle grammar-carrying final consonants (-ed, -s, -t).
   - Flag s-cluster starts (strategy, specific, Spain) to avoid adding "e-".
2. **3-run rehearsal (aloud):**
   - Run 1: read aloud from the marked script, focusing on chunking and pauses.
   - Run 2: speak from keyword notes and record audio or video.
   - Run 3: deliver to a live listener or on a call, with a real person if possible (exposure).
   - Review the recording once for *one* target, such as focus words, rather than everything at once.
3. **Imagined vs actual check:** before watching the recording, rate "how nervous did I look?" (0–10), then watch and re-rate. This mirrors the Telch-lab mechanism.
4. **Opening/closing:** rehearse the first 2–3 sentences and the closing line aloud until fluent. This is expert practice (the reduced cognitive load rationale from Abrahams), not tested directly.
5. **Q&A drill:** a partner asks 5 likely questions plus 1 surprise. Answer each with "What? So what? Now what?" within 60 seconds. Repeat with recording.
6. **Pre-talk 3-minute routine:**
   - Exhale-long breathing (cyclic sighing) for ~1–2 minutes.
   - "I'm excited" aloud.
   - Name-based self-talk.
   - A short vocal warm-up.
7. **Pace calibration:** record a 1-minute segment. If listeners struggle, first add pauses at thought-group boundaries before slowing syllables. Too-slow speech also reduces comprehensibility (Munro & Derwing 2001).

### Gaps
- **Notes strategy:** I found no experimental evidence comparing keyword notes vs full script vs memorization for presentation quality. This remains expert opinion (e.g. Toastmasters guidance, which was not retrieved).
- **Memorizing openings and closings:** I found no direct study. Abrahams' sources retrieved here did not explicitly recommend memorizing openings.
- **Spaced or distributed rehearsal:** spaced practice for speeches specifically was not researched here. The general spacing-effect literature was not retrieved.
- Toastmasters materials and Vinh Giang's claims were not retrieved or verified. Vinh Giang content should be treated as entertainment/expert opinion until checked.

## 5. Body language and voice on video calls; eye contact with the camera

### Takeaway
Evidence on camera eye contact is thin and mixed. Lack of mutual gaze on video is linked to lower trust, but a 2025 mock-interview study found simulated eye contact had little effect on interviewer evaluations. Looking slightly *below* the lens may create the best impression of eye contact, though that comes from a small poster study.

### Cited Findings
- **[EVIDENCE, small]** A 2025 Virginia Tech study on simulated eye contact in video interviews found it had little impact on evaluations "compared to common belief." The authors warn that gaze-correction tools can be deceptive. — [VTechWorks](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/items/2259b66d-8f96-46b2-9a97-d10045f55cdc)
- **[WEAK]** VSS 2024 poster (4 actors, 17 raters): looking ~2° below the camera appears ideal for being perceived as making eye contact. — [Vision Sciences Society](https://www.visionsciences.org/presentation/?id=1094)
- **[EVIDENCE, older/secondary]** The Otago HCI group argues that the lack of eye contact in videoconferencing reduces communication quality, empathy and trust, citing Bekkering & Shim (2006) on lack of trust. That citation is secondhand. — [Regenbrecht et al. (PDF)](https://www.hci.otago.ac.nz/papers/RegenbrechtOZCHI2014B.pdf); [Regenbrecht CAIS 2015 (PDF)](https://www.hci.otago.ac.nz/papers/RegenbrechtCAIS2015.pdf)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Stanford's Lemann Center reports research on how gaze, distance from camera and camera angle affect viewers' impressions on video calls. Findings were not retrieved. — [Stanford Lemann Center](https://lemanncenter.stanford.edu/node/704)
- **[WEAK, vendor]** Nguyen & Canny (2007) is reported by a camera vendor to show that gaze-preserving video produced trust comparable to face-to-face. This is a vendor summary. — [iContact Camera blog](https://icontactcamera.com/blogs/research/facilitating-trust-in-video-chats-with-eye-contact)

### Inferences
- Practical advice: place the camera at eye level and the speaker-notes or video window directly under the lens. Look at the lens (or just below) at key moments: the opening, the main point, the closing and the ask. There is no need to stare at it the whole time.
- Since pace and pauses drive comprehensibility, and audio quality on calls may worsen L2 comprehension (noise reduced comprehension in the 2007 TTS study), a good microphone is likely a higher priority than camera tricks for L2 presenters. This inference is extrapolated from the TTS noise condition.

### Gaps
- I found no rigorous research on vocal delivery specifically on video calls for L2 speakers.
- I found no peer-reviewed evidence on gestures and framing on video for perceived competence. The Lemann Center study details were not retrieved.
- Bailenson's "Zoom fatigue" (Stanford VHIL) work on self-view and gaze was not researched here.
