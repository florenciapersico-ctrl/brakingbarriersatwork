# Interview presentations: what panels evaluate, why candidates fail, and how to handle the brief

Scope: the "presentation as part of the interview" format (30-60-90 day plan, case/strategy presentation, "how would you approach X", "a project you led", panel presentation of 10–20 min + Q&A). Audience: Spanish-speaking mid/senior professional women at LATAM multinationals, presenting in English as non-native speakers.

Evidence labels used below:
- **[RUBRIC/OFFICIAL]**: published employer framework or official guidance
- **[EVIDENCE]**: peer-reviewed or empirical research
- **[RECRUITER]**: recruitment/executive-search firm advice (expert opinion, often promotional)
- **[CAREER SITE]**: career content site or prep vendor (expert opinion, weakest)

---

## 1. Why companies use interview presentations and what competencies they assess

### Takeaway
Employers use presentations as a job-related simulation, a work sample, to see evidence rather than claims. Panels assess four things together: (a) thinking and judgement (understanding of the role and business, prioritisation, a clear recommendation); (b) structure and organisation; (c) communication pitched to the audience; and (d) composure under challenge in the Q&A, which several sources say weighs as much as the talk itself. At senior level, panels watch for "future leader" signals: whether the candidate can make a call under scrutiny and carry a senior room.

### Cited Findings
- **[RUBRIC/OFFICIAL]** The UK Civil Service Success Profiles list presentations under "How we assess behaviours", as a "job-related simulation" used alongside oral briefings and role-plays. Presentations are a method for assessing behaviours, not a behaviour in their own right. Page updated 29 Jan 2025. — [GOV.UK Success Profiles: Civil Service Behaviours](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/success-profiles/success-profiles-civil-service-behaviours)
- **[RUBRIC/OFFICIAL]** In the Civil Service, recruiting managers choose a subset of behaviours for each role, and "you will not be asked to demonstrate all" of them. The job advert says which elements are assessed and how, so the advert is effectively the scoring key. — [GOV.UK Success Profiles candidate overview](https://gov.uk/government/publications/success-profiles/success-profiles-candidate-overview)
- **[RUBRIC/OFFICIAL]** Behaviour indicators that a presentation exercise typically evidences, by grade:
  - *Communicating and Influencing*, Grade 6/7 (mid/senior manager): "Explain complex issues in a way that is easy to understand"; "Deliver difficult messages with clarity and sensitivity, being persuasive when required".
  - Deputy Director: "Communicate with conviction and clarity in the face of tough negotiations or challenges".
  - Director/DG: "Overcome objections to gain acceptance of the vision".
  - *Seeing the Big Picture*, G6/7: make sure plans "reflect wider strategic priorities and influence senior leaders".
  - Deputy Director: "Anticipate the long-term effects of major developments". — [GOV.UK Civil Service Behaviours](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/success-profiles/success-profiles-civil-service-behaviours)
- **[RECRUITER]** Hays (Karen Young, Director, Hays Accountancy & Finance UK&I, undated) says employers want "proof of your abilities". Presentations assess abilities required by the job, written and verbal communication, diligence, organisation, and commitment/enthusiasm. They are "becoming more common as candidates move into senior roles" but are now used from entry level up. — [Hays](https://www.hays.com/career-advice/article/four-tips-for-your-interview-presentation)
- **[RECRUITER]** Reed (Olivia Maguire, 4 Jun 2021) says presentations give "a broader view of your general aptitude" than a Q&A or competency interview. Effort signals "how much you want this position". Panels assess knowledge, experience, communication, organisation, attention to detail (spelling and grammar), commercial awareness, brand awareness (using the company's colours and fonts), and handling of challenging questions, where "how you react is often more important than the answer itself". — [Reed](https://www.reed.com/articles/how-to-prepare-for-an-interview-presentation)
- **[RECRUITER]** Fraser Dove International, an executive search firm in life sciences (Joseph Brown, CCO; published 2023, updated June 2026), says the panel is "assessing you as a future leader", not marking a deck. Panels look for evidence of each JD competency (strategic thinking, commercial judgement, leading under pressure) and for judgement: weighing evidence, making a call under scrutiny, and carrying a senior room. — [Fraser Dove](https://fraserdove.com/insights/executive-interview-presentation/)
- **[CAREER SITE]** The Muse says a presentation can show public speaking skills, knowledge of a topic, and "ability to stay calm under pressure". Sticking to the assigned format shows you can manage time, respect your audience and follow directions. These points come from search-result summaries; the pages were not fetched in full. — [The Muse, 5 steps](https://themuse.com/advice/5-steps-to-acing-your-interview-presentation); [The Muse, 7 tips](https://www.themuse.com/advice/7-tips-to-help-you-nail-that-interview-presentation)
- **[RECRUITER]** For 30-60-90 plans, ManpowerGroup and career sources say managers use the plan to check whether you understand the job and its duties and whether you can perform them, which makes you look a "safe hire". — [ManpowerGroup](https://workforce-resources.manpowergroup.com/blog/answers-to-common-interview-questions-what-would-you-do-in-your-first-90-days-in-this-position); [ManpowerGroup, 30-60-90](https://workforce-resources.manpowergroup.com/blog/creating-a-30-60-90-day-plan-to-secure-the-job)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Background on why simulations are used: Sackett et al.'s reanalysis of selection-method validity (Journal of Applied Psychology, 2022) lowered earlier estimates. Approximate revised figures:
  - Work samples: about .33 (was .54)
  - Assessment centres: about .29 (was .37)
  - Structured interviews: about .42, now the top predictor

  These figures come from secondary summaries of the paper, so verify them against the original. Work samples and assessment centres are still useful predictors, but they are not the near-perfect predictors older HR literature claimed. — [Master-HR summary](https://master-hr.com/insights/new-study-providing-updated-validity-estimates/); [TestGorilla summary](https://testgorilla.com/blog/hiring-tools-validity-revisited)
- **[CAREER SITE]** Assessment-centre practice:
  - Assessors use ORCE: Observe, Record, Classify, Evaluate. They write down what the candidate said and did and score only from that evidence, usually 1–5 per competency per exercise.
  - A "wash-up" meeting combines all scores into a matrix and moderates discrepancies.
  - Candidates are measured against a benchmark, not against each other.
  - The competencies typically assessed are communication, teamwork, problem-solving, organisation, influence and resilience.

  Source: Ingmar van Maurik, prep vendor, 13 Aug 2026. — [Assessment-Training blog](https://blog.assessment-training.com/interview-questions/assessment-centres)

### Inferences
- Because assessors score observed evidence against pre-chosen criteria (ORCE, Success Profiles), the most effective preparation is to map each slide or section explicitly to the JD or person-specification criteria. If a competency is not visibly evidenced, it cannot be scored.
- Presentations evidence two separate things: the *content* (thinking and judgement) and the *behaviour* (communication, composure). A strong deck with weak Q&A, or the reverse, will score unevenly.
- The panel is also judging a work behaviour: how the candidate would present to *them* as a colleague or boss. Tailoring to their level is itself evidence.

### Gaps
- I found no survey data on what share of employers use presentation interviews, or how they weight the presentation against other stages.
- I found no study validating interview presentations specifically, as opposed to work samples or assessment centres in general.
- I did not find published practice from Korn Ferry, Spencer Stuart or Heidrick & Struggles on presentation interviews.

## 2. Published rubrics and assessment criteria

### Takeaway
Few employers publish a presentation-specific rubric. What does exist is (a) competency frameworks with behavioural indicators, scored on numeric scales (UK Civil Service; NHS panel formats), and (b) generic panel scoring grids with weighted criteria (universities). Across sources, the de facto rubric is:
- Meeting the brief
- Content relevance and depth (commercial or role understanding)
- Structure and organisation, including time management
- Communication and audience tailoring
- Use of visual aids
- Q&A handling

### Cited Findings
- **[RUBRIC/OFFICIAL]** UK Civil Service: elements and behaviours are chosen per role and stated in the advert. Behaviour indicators by grade are quoted in Q1. — [GOV.UK Success Profiles candidate overview](https://gov.uk/government/publications/success-profiles/success-profiles-candidate-overview); [GOV.UK Behaviours](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/success-profiles/success-profiles-civil-service-behaviours)
- **[RUBRIC/OFFICIAL]** Other departments' versions of the same frameworks list "presentation and in-tray exercise" among ways of assessing behaviours. — [DE&S / MOD Success Profile Behaviours 2025 PDF](https://des.mod.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/20250527-Success-Profile-Behaviours-2025-document-v1-O.pdf); [gov.scot candidate guide](https://gov.scot/publications/success-profiles-candidate-guide/pages/behaviours)
- **[RUBRIC/OFFICIAL]** NHS example (Higher Specialist Scientist Training): a 10-minute candidate presentation, then 10 minutes of questions *on the presentation*, then separate blocks on leadership and specialist science. The panel is two specialty scientists, an HEI/NSHCS representative and an external observer. — [NSHCS HSST interview panel member](https://nshcs.hee.nhs.uk/get-involved/roles/hsst-interview-panel-member/)
- **[RUBRIC/OFFICIAL]** NHS Tayside: panels are usually two or more interviewers, presentations are one possible method, and length and method depend on the post. — [NHS Tayside interview information](https://www.nhstayside.scot.nhs.uk/WorkingWithUs/InterviewInformation/PROD_243080)
- **[RUBRIC/OFFICIAL]** Cardiff University interview scoring grid: each panel member scores separately against person-specification criteria. Questions are weighted 1 (lower importance) or 2 (higher), and totals are summed for the decision. — [Cardiff University scoring grid PDF](https://www.cardiff.ac.uk/__data/assets/pdf_file/0004/754537/Interview_scoring_grid.pdf)
- **[RUBRIC/OFFICIAL]** University of Illinois Chicago academic-hiring guidance: scoring sheets require a structured plan, and panels must "put the meaning of each potential score into words" (1–5 anchors, from "misses the question" to "excellent") rather than rely on intuition. — [UIC Developing Interview Criteria](https://oae.uic.edu/ah/academic-hiring-process/search-resources/interview-criteria/)
- **[CAREER SITE]** Typical presentation-exercise dimensions in assessment centres:
  - Communication: speaking, articulating ideas, tailoring to the audience
  - Use of visual aids
  - Organisation of material and time management
  - Accuracy, relevance and depth of content
  - Q&A, often scored separately and running 10–15 minutes

  Marks are given both "for achieving the brief and for how well they present". Assessors score clarity of thinking rather than slide design. — [AssessmentDay](https://www.assessmentday.co.uk/assessmentcentre/presentations.html); [CareerTestPrep](https://www.careertestprep.com/knowledge/presentation-interview); [GovJobs](https://govjobs.uk/blog/assessment-centres-public-sector) (summarised from search results; these are vendor guides, not official rubrics)
- **[CAREER SITE]** One vendor says panels are often three or more people, usually at least one grade above the role. — [CareerTestPrep](https://www.careertestprep.com/knowledge/presentation-interview) (search-result summary)
- **[CAREER SITE]** The Q&A is "scored as heavily as the presentation". Acknowledging a fair challenge and explaining how you would test the assumption is credited, while bluffing "collapse[s] under one follow-up question". — [Assessment-Training blog](https://blog.assessment-training.com/interview-questions/assessment-centres)

### Inferences
- A practical rubric a candidate can self-score against, synthesised from the sources above:
  1. Answered the brief
  2. Clear recommendation or point of view
  3. Logical structure
  4. Evidence of company and role research
  5. Realistic, specific actions
  6. Audience-appropriate language
  7. On time
  8. Clean visuals
  9. Composed, non-defensive Q&A
  10. Clear close or ask

  Each item maps to at least one cited source.
- In public-sector and assessment-centre settings, the advert or candidate pack is the closest thing to the rubric. Candidates should mirror its exact competency wording.

### Gaps
- I found no published private-sector or executive-search presentation scoring rubric (Korn Ferry, Spencer Stuart, Heidrick, Hays). These firms publish tips, not scoring sheets.
- I found no official NHS or Civil Service *presentation-specific* marking scheme, only frameworks and formats.

## 3. What distinguishes the best presentations, and the most-cited mistakes

### Takeaway
The best presentations lead with a recommendation and a few reasons, are tailored to this company's real priorities, keep to time, use sparse slides, and treat the Q&A as a two-way conversation. The most-cited failures follow from that:
- Misreading or not answering the brief
- Generic or templated content, including AI-built decks
- No company research
- Background first, conclusion buried
- Text-heavy decks
- Overrunning, or finishing far too early
- Reading slides or a script
- Overpromising in 30-60-90 plans
- Bluffing or defensiveness under challenge
- No backup for tech failure

### Cited Findings
**What top candidates do**
- **[RECRUITER]** Lead with your recommendation and three reasons, not background. Use one message per slide with 3–5 points, and an 80:20 split of informative to thought-provoking content. Tailor to sector priorities (e.g., pharma panels weight regulatory and commercial thinking). Cite credible sources. — [Fraser Dove](https://fraserdove.com/insights/executive-interview-presentation/)
- **[CAREER SITE]** The panel marks clarity and organisation "not theatre". The recommended structure is recommendation, three supporting reasons, risks, then next steps. — [Assessment-Training blog](https://blog.assessment-training.com/interview-questions/assessment-centres)
- **[RECRUITER]** Build the talk from the JD and person specification. Research company objectives and recent industry or company changes. Open with an overview slide and a hook (fact, quote or statistic), give each slide a headline plus a visual or a few bullets, and summarise key messages at the end. — [Hays](https://www.hays.com/career-advice/article/four-tips-for-your-interview-presentation)
- **[RECRUITER]** Reed's practical guidance:
  - No more than 10 slides, one idea each, minimum 24-pt font, the "4x6 rule"
  - Default to about 10 minutes including questions if no length is given
  - Include benefits *and limitations* of your suggestions
  - Restate the brief at the start

  — [Reed](https://www.reed.com/articles/how-to-prepare-for-an-interview-presentation)
- **[RECRUITER]** For senior roles, cover risks and alternatives, including what you are explicitly *not* recommending, because senior leaders will raise them anyway. If you disagree, say so once with your reasoning, then defer. — [Moxie Institute](https://www.moxieinstitute.com/10-tips-for-impressive-presentations-to-senior-leadership-and-executives/) (search-result summary)
- **[RECRUITER]** Pick one or two challenges the business likely faces and give a structured, high-level approach. A detailed plan is not needed, but judgement and commercial awareness are. Express results in revenue, margin, cost or strategic positioning. — [CSG Talent](https://www.csgtalent.com/insights/blog/executive-interview-preparation-2026-senior-leadership-tips/) (search-result summary; CSG cites statistics such as "47% of interviewers reject for lack of company knowledge" with no traceable source, so do not use them)
- **[RECRUITER/CAREER SITE]** For 30-60-90 plans:
  - Customise to the employer, because "a generic plan defeats the purpose"
  - Identify the organisation's top three strategic initiatives
  - Keep actions to what you can control, and avoid "imaginary targets"
  - Keep it short (brief deck or a 1–3 page document)
  - Use it to open a discussion, not to deliver a verdict

  — [ManpowerGroup](https://workforce-resources.manpowergroup.com/blog/creating-a-30-60-90-day-plan-to-secure-the-job); [Work It Daily](https://www.workitdaily.com/getting-job-offer-30-60-90-day-plan)

**Most-cited mistakes**
- **Not answering the brief.** Reed lists presenting something other than the brief. Fraser Dove names "misreading the brief" as a top mistake. Assessment centres give marks specifically "for achieving the brief". — [Reed](https://www.reed.com/articles/how-to-prepare-for-an-interview-presentation); [Fraser Dove](https://fraserdove.com/insights/executive-interview-presentation/); [AssessmentDay](https://www.assessmentday.co.uk/assessmentcentre/presentations.html)
- **Burying the point.** Opening with background or company history instead of the recommendation, and saving the key point for late in the deck. — [Fraser Dove](https://fraserdove.com/insights/executive-interview-presentation/). Note a conflict: Reed's suggested 10-slide template still puts "about you" and "company history" before the answer, so the recruiter sources disagree on order. — [Reed](https://www.reed.com/articles/how-to-prepare-for-an-interview-presentation)
- **Generic or AI-built deck.** Fraser Dove warns against "a generic, AI-built deck": use AI for drafts and practice, but the judgement and examples must be your own. — [Fraser Dove](https://fraserdove.com/insights/executive-interview-presentation/)
- **Insufficient company research.** A Michael Page director calls failing to prepare one of the biggest errors. Skimming the website misses the culture and the "challenges the company is currently facing". — [Michael Page UK](https://www.michaelpage.co.uk/advice/career-advice/job-interview-tips/five-interview-mistakes-you-should-never-make)
- **Too much content.** 30-slide decks, text blocks, crowded slides and jargon. — [Reed](https://www.reed.com/articles/how-to-prepare-for-an-interview-presentation); [Hays](https://www.hays.com/career-advice/article/four-tips-for-your-interview-presentation)
- **Time.** Exceeding the limit "may suggest poor time management" (Reed). Finishing early suggests a shallow argument, and running over suggests weak preparation and risks being cut off (Fraser Dove). — [Reed](https://www.reed.com/articles/how-to-prepare-for-an-interview-presentation); [Fraser Dove](https://fraserdove.com/insights/executive-interview-presentation/)
- **Reading slides or a script, or turning your back to the panel.** Use cue cards as prompts only. — [Reed](https://www.reed.com/articles/how-to-prepare-for-an-interview-presentation); [Hays](https://www.hays.com/career-advice/article/four-tips-for-your-interview-presentation)
- **Eye contact with only one panelist.** Maintain eye contact with the whole panel, not just the person who set the task. — [WikiJob](https://www.wikijob.co.uk/application-advice/assessment-centres/presentation) (search-result summary)
- **Defensive or bluffing Q&A.** Candidates who present well but turn defensive under questioning can finish below those with a weaker talk and a composed Q&A. — [CareerTestPrep](https://www.careertestprep.com/knowledge/presentation-interview) (search-result summary). On bluffing: "If you can't answer, don't bluff. Say you will follow up." — [Fraser Dove](https://fraserdove.com/insights/executive-interview-presentation/); [Assessment-Training blog](https://blog.assessment-training.com/interview-questions/assessment-centres)
- **30-60-90 specific.** Overpromising results in 90 days (managers know what a new hire can achieve), vagueness, ignoring the role's main objectives, no measurable milestones, and too much or too little detail. — [SlideUpLift](https://slideuplift.com/blog/30-60-90-day-plan-for-interview/); [InterviewPal](https://www.interviewpal.com/blog/the-30-60-90-interview-question-what-it-is-and-how-to-ace-it) (career sites, search-result summaries)
- **"We" instead of "I".** Particularly relevant to "tell us about a project you led": candidates describe team successes as "we", which makes their individual contribution hard to judge. — [Michael Page UK](https://www.michaelpage.co.uk/advice/career-advice/job-interview-tips/five-interview-mistakes-you-should-never-make)
- **Tech failure without backup.** Recommended backups: email the file to yourself and the recruiter, carry a USB copy, bring printed handouts for each panelist, and have a PDF ready for video calls. If tech fails, keep going without slides. — [Hays](https://www.hays.com/career-advice/article/four-tips-for-your-interview-presentation); [Reed](https://www.reed.com/articles/how-to-prepare-for-an-interview-presentation); [Fraser Dove](https://fraserdove.com/insights/executive-interview-presentation/)
- **Recovering mid-talk.** If you go blank, take a sip of water and repeat your last point. If you lose your thread, pause and check your notes; "a short silence feels longer to you than to the panel". — [Reed](https://www.reed.com/articles/how-to-prepare-for-an-interview-presentation); [Fraser Dove](https://fraserdove.com/insights/executive-interview-presentation/)
- **Q&A preparation.** List the three toughest challenges to your position and prepare a two-sentence answer to each. Decide in advance which questions you will answer in writing afterwards. Expect to be asked to skip slides. — [Fraser Dove](https://fraserdove.com/insights/executive-interview-presentation/)
- **Follow-up.** Offer the deck through the recruiter within about 24 hours, with a note restating one or two key points and clarifying any incomplete answers. — [Fraser Dove](https://fraserdove.com/insights/executive-interview-presentation/)

### Inferences
- Most failures trace to two root causes: (1) treating the task as "show everything I know" instead of "answer this brief for this audience", and (2) treating the Q&A as an attack rather than as part of the scored exercise.
- The single most portable structure across sources is: restate brief, then recommendation, then 3 reasons with evidence, then risks and what I'd validate first, then next steps or ask, then Q&A.

### Gaps
- None of the "most common mistakes" lists rests on survey data of hiring managers. All are practitioner opinion, and I found no ranking of mistakes by frequency.
- I could not confirm specific YouTube coaches' (Andrew LaCivita, Linda Raynier, Jeff Su) views on interview presentations. Searches surfaced only LaCivita's general podcast advice (e.g., that rehearsing common answers alone is a poor approach), nothing presentation-specific. — [LaCivita podcast](https://andrewlacivita.libsyn.com/podcast/5-steps-to-improve-your-job-interview-performance)
- HBR searches returned no relevant HBR article on interview presentations, only unofficial PDF copies of the HBR Guide to Presentations, which I excluded.

## 4. How to clarify an ambiguous brief

### Takeaway
Every recruiter source advises clarifying the brief, through the recruiter or the hiring contact, before building anything. The questions to ask are largely the same across Hays, Reed, The Muse and Fraser Dove. Restating your interpretation of the brief at the start of the presentation is a second safeguard.

### Cited Findings
- **[RECRUITER]** Fraser Dove's list of things to confirm:
  - Whether visual aids are allowed, and in what format and with what equipment
  - Total length, and whether questions are included
  - Panel members and their roles
  - Whether the topic is set or chosen by you
  - In person or video, and what technology is available

  — [Fraser Dove](https://fraserdove.com/insights/executive-interview-presentation/)
- **[RECRUITER]** Recruiters are in regular contact with interviewers and can clarify the task and how it relates to the role. Confirm length and format, and whether you'll use their devices or your own. — [Hays](https://www.hays.com/career-advice/article/four-tips-for-your-interview-presentation)
- **[RECRUITER]** Ask your recruiter about a brief you don't understand, since they may have seen it before. If dealing directly with the hiring manager, check your interpretation with them. Restate the brief on your first slide. — [Reed](https://www.reed.com/articles/how-to-prepare-for-an-interview-presentation)
- **[CAREER SITE]** The Muse suggests asking how long the talk should be, whether they want to see particular skills, how many people will attend, and what the room's slide and IT setup is. — [The Muse, 7 tips](https://www.themuse.com/advice/7-tips-to-help-you-nail-that-interview-presentation) (search-result summary)
- **[CAREER SITE]** If no topic is named, ask whether the team wants a specific subject or your own choice. Ask about the panel's expertise so you can pitch the level and keep jargon down. — [Indeed](https://www.indeed.com/career-advice/interviewing/deliver-presentation-in-interview) (search-result summary; the page returned 403 when fetched)
- **[CAREER SITE]** Keep take-home assignments to a reasonable scope; "most job interview assignments should only require a few hours". — [Indeed, interview assignments](https://www.indeed.com/career-advice/interviewing/job-interview-assignment-example) (search-result summary)
- **[RUBRIC/OFFICIAL]** In public-sector processes, the job advert and candidate pack state which competencies are assessed. Reasonable adjustments, such as extra time, can be requested at application. — [GOV.UK Success Profiles candidate overview](https://gov.uk/government/publications/success-profiles/success-profiles-candidate-overview)
- **[RECRUITER]** Research panel members' roles, achievements and publications (without connecting with them on LinkedIn) and predict their likely questions. — [Fraser Dove](https://fraserdove.com/insights/executive-interview-presentation/)

### Inferences
Consolidated question checklist for the recruiter or hiring contact, derived from the sources above:
1. What exactly is the question or brief? Is the topic fixed or my choice?
2. How long in total, and how much of that is presentation versus Q&A?
3. Who is on the panel (names, roles, functions, seniority)? Will the hiring manager be there?
4. Which competencies or criteria are they assessing? Is there a scoring framework?
5. Are slides required, optional or not allowed? Should I send the deck in advance, and by when?
6. In person or video? Which platform? My laptop or theirs? Is there a clicker or screen?
7. Will I be interrupted during the talk, or are questions held to the end?
8. Can I share any data or context with you? Which assumptions should I make?
9. For a senior role: what does success look like at 12 months? (Skyhigh frames this as a risk question at senior level.) — [Skyhigh Coaching](https://www.skyhighcoaching.com.au/post/executive-interview-tips-for-senior-leaders-and-directors) (search-result summary)

Where ambiguity remains, state your assumptions explicitly on a slide. This ties to the "acknowledge and explain how you'd test the assumption" behaviour that assessors credit (see Q2).

For non-native speakers specifically, three further questions are worth asking. These are my own inference, not found in any source:
- Can I send the slides in advance?
- Will I be able to share my screen myself?
- Will the panel include non-native speakers or people from other regions?

Sending slides in advance lets listeners pre-process the content, which may reduce the comprehension load that research links to accent penalties (see Q5).

### Gaps
- I found no source on how often candidates ask clarifying questions, or whether asking is ever penalised. All sources assume it is welcome.

## 5. What differs for senior and leadership roles (plus non-native speaker considerations)

### Takeaway
At senior level the panel scores leadership judgement, commercial framing and presence under challenge more than polish. Expect fewer slides, a recommendation up front, explicit risks and trade-offs, more interruptions ("skip to slide 6"), and Q&A that probes your reasoning. For non-native English speakers, peer-reviewed research shows accent can lower perceived credibility and employability when speech is harder to process. The mechanism is listener processing fluency, not competence, which means clarity, structure and signposting partly offset it.

### Cited Findings
**Senior and leadership roles**
- **[RECRUITER]** Presentations are "becoming more common as candidates move into senior roles". — [Hays](https://www.hays.com/career-advice/article/four-tips-for-your-interview-presentation)
- **[RECRUITER]** The executive panel is "assessing you as a future leader": judgement, weighing evidence, making a call under scrutiny. Senior panelists may pick holes in your logic or ask you to skip slides. Plan which slides you can drop, and keep backup data ready. — [Fraser Dove](https://fraserdove.com/insights/executive-interview-presentation/)
- **[RECRUITER]** At senior level, show judgement and commercial awareness with a high-level approach to one or two real business challenges, and quantify impact (revenue, margin, cost, positioning). — [CSG Talent](https://www.csgtalent.com/insights/blog/executive-interview-preparation-2026-senior-leadership-tips/) (search-result summary)
- **[RECRUITER]** Start with the decision you want and what the room needs to do. Address risks and what you are not recommending. Disagree once, with reasons, then defer. — [Moxie Institute](https://www.moxieinstitute.com/10-tips-for-impressive-presentations-to-senior-leadership-and-executives/) (search-result summary)
- **[RECRUITER]** Senior interviews are framed around risk. Ask what would make the panel feel they made the right appointment 12 months out. **Non-native speakers of English**
- **[EVIDENCE]** Lev-Ari & Keysar (2010, *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 46(6):1093–1096):
  - Trivia statements read by non-native speakers were rated less truthful.
  - Mild accents were rated close to native; heavy accents were rated lower.
  - The effect persisted even when listeners were told about it.
  - The authors attribute it to *processing fluency*: harder-to-process speech feels less credible. They explicitly suggest implications for non-native job seekers.

  — [UChicago News](https://news.uchicago.edu/story/foreign-accents-make-speakers-seem-less-truthful-listeners-research-shows); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/speakers-foreign-accent-are-perceived-less-credible)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Replications are mixed. A 2021 follow-up (Boduch-Grabka & Lev-Ari) replicated the penalty but found it shrank after listeners were exposed to the accent, and other studies failed to find the effect. — [Journal of Cognition](https://journalofcognition.org/en/articles/10.5334/joc.353) (search-result summary; check the exact paper)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Timming (2017, *Work, Employment and Society* 31(3):409–428):
  - US managers in simulated telephone interviews rated Chinese-, Mexican- and Indian-accented applicants less favourably.
  - The penalty was larger for customer-facing roles.
  - British-accented applicants did as well as or better than native American speakers.

  Mexican-accented English is directly relevant to this audience. — [St Andrews repository](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/handle/10023/8724); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/woemps/v31y2017i3p409-428.html)
- **[EVIDENCE, weak]** Several small Dutch bachelor/master theses show mixed results. In one, Spanish-accented speakers were rated *higher* than American speakers on some factors. Another found accent *strength*, rather than accent per se, drove the hireability penalty, and the effect was "relatively minimal". Another found the accent–hiring link mediated by understandability, similarity and interpersonal attraction. — [Radboud thesis repository](https://theses.ubn.ru.nl/items/df1c06b4-204e-4144-b0f4-deaef4d6e386/full); [Radboud thesis](https://theses.ubn.ru.nl/handle/123456789/11842); [IIT repository](https://repository.iit.edu/islandora/object/islandora%3A6700/datastream/DC/download)

### Inferences
- **Mechanism.** Because the evidence points to *understandability* and processing fluency, rather than accent itself, as the driver, the most useful moves for non-native presenters are ones that reduce the listener's processing effort:
  - A clear signposted structure ("three points: first…")
  - Recommendation first
  - Short sentences
  - Slides with the key words the speaker will say, so listeners can read and hear them at once
  - Slower pace with pauses, which Reed and the WikiJob summary already advise for everyone
  - Sending the deck in advance

  The same moves are also generic best practice, so there is no trade-off. This is inference, not tested in the cited studies.
- **Q&A.** For a non-native speaker, the Q&A is the riskiest segment, since it is unscripted and assessors weight it heavily. Preparing two-sentence answers to the likely tough questions (Fraser Dove) and having phrases ready to buy time and clarify ("Let me make sure I understand the question…", "I'd want to validate that assumption by…") is especially valuable. Asking for clarification is explicitly endorsed by Reed.
- **Senior women from LATAM.** The senior-role emphasis on stating a clear recommendation and "disagreeing once, with reasons" may run against communication norms some candidates learned. This is inference, and I found no source on gender or cultural differences in interview-presentation scoring.
- **"We" versus "I".** Collectivist framing ("we") is common in Spanish-language professional culture and is flagged by Michael Page as making individual contribution hard to judge. This is particularly relevant for "tell us about a project you led". The cultural link is inference; the Michael Page point is sourced.
