These are sentences you actually said. Cover the correction, find the mistake and say the full sentence again. Then decide: Gap (something new to learn) or Fossilized (you know it, but it comes out wrong under pressure).

What you said The mistake Correction Gap or Fossilized?

"The only thing that confusing me is…"that confusing meThe only thing that confuses me is…

→ Gap identified: indirect questions. We work on it in Block 2.

Two rules to remember:
1 · After "to", the bare verb. I need to prepare · I wouldn't want to spend · the goal is to install. Never to preparing.
2 · In an indirect question, the subject comes first. What are the requirements? → I'd like to know what the requirements are.

Now say these:

  1. I need ______ (confirm) the Wi-Fi before the rollout.
  2. I wouldn't want ______ (buy) the sensors twice.
  3. The part that ______ (worry) me is the Wi-Fi.

2 · Language Clinic · Indirect questions (10 min)

A direct question turns the words around: What is the cost? When you put it inside a polite sentence, you turn them back: I'd like to know what the cost is. Nothing else changes: no do, no does, no inversion.

DirectInside a polite sentence
What are the requirements?I'd like to know what the requirements are.
Where is the meeting?Do you know where the meeting is?
How much does it cost?Could you tell me how much it costs?
Do we need a VM?I'd like to know whether we need a VM.

Openers you can use at work: I'd like to know… · Could you tell me… · Do you know… · I was wondering… · Can you confirm…

Say it out loud. Turn each one into a polite sentence:

  1. What are the device requirements? → I'd like to know ______________________.
  2. Is the Wi-Fi effective in the yard? → Could you tell me ______________________.
  3. How many cameras do we need? → I'd like to know ______________________.
  4. When will the software go live? → Do you know ______________________.
  5. Who is going to fund the hardware? → Can you confirm ______________________.

3 · Skill · How English Really Sounds (20 min)

Written English and spoken English are not the same. In speech, words join and the small ones get squeezed. That's why you understand a transcript and not the call. Today we look at the real thing.

StepWhat you do
1 · ListenFlor reads it at real speed. No subtitles. Just listen.
2 · Main pointOne sentence: what does he want?
3 · Spot the joinsSecond time. Mark ⌣ where two words become one sound.
4 · Write what you hearThird time. Write the exact words, even the small ones.
5 · Say itSay it back with the same joins. Not word by word.

Scenario A · Your regional colleague (his real words, 18 Sep meeting)

  1. "You probably would want to do your evaluation before you spend any money."
  2. "Because if you start buying dock sensors and then find out that you don't need them, that's an expense that didn't need to happen."
  3. "I'm not trying to influence the site. You guys can pick whatever direction you want."
  4. "All of those types of questions are very solid and can and should be worked in advance of the software."

What's really happening in his mouth:

WrittenWhat you hearWhy
would want to"wud-WANNA"want to → wanna
don't need them"doan-NEED-əm"the th of them disappears
didn't need to"DIDN-need-tə"to is never "too", it's /tə/
all of those"ALL-ə-those"of → /əv/ → /ə/
in advance of"in-ad-VANCE-əv"the stress is in the middle of the word
can and should be"kən-ən-shud-bi"can and and lose their vowel

The rule: the words that carry meaning stay strong (evaluation, money, sensors, requirements). The glue words get squeezed (to, of, and, them, can, a). English has a beat, and the glue words fall between the beats.
